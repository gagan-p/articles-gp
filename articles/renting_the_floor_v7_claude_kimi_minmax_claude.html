Renting the Floor

India has the world's largest developer base and one of its smallest outputs of foundational software. China, from the same starting point, chose otherwise.

Core thesis

Value, durability, and strategic leverage in software accrue to whoever owns the foundational floor — the databases, brokers, meshes, consensus systems, kernels, and runtimes that everything else is built upon — and a nation strong only in the layer above is a tenant, however large its developer base. Floors come into existence under two conditions, both necessary: a wall that cannot be rented around, and an institution independent of the builder that takes custody of the result so that it stays and spreads. China was handed the first — import substitution, export controls, and a billion users at once — and built the second, donating its systems software to the Apache and CNCF commons. India has had neither: its firms could always buy their way around every wall, rationally, and the foundational work its engineers did build has had nowhere to live.

The sharpest form of the claim is not that India declined to build the floor. Hasura, YugabyteDB, and MinIO are floor-level software with Indian founders at their origin, and the custody of all three now sits in the United States. India has no institution that keeps the floor it builds.

The unit that matters is therefore the entity, not the passport and not the office address. A contribution builds national capability only when an Indian entity owns the result and holds it where others can build upon it. Skill in Indian hands that accrues to a foreign balance sheet is not a national asset; it is an export of the asset at the moment of its creation. By that measure India’s record is not thin but empty, and the deficit it exposes is custody rather than capability.

In brief
  • India has the world’s largest developer base and the largest open-source contributor base by headcount, having overtaken the United States for the first time.
  • Its output is application-layer and built for export: US$283bn in revenue, 79% of it sold abroad, and almost no systems software owned.
  • Value in software accrues to whoever owns the foundational floor that other software depends upon.
  • Measured by market value per employee, the firms that own the floor are worth one hundred to one thousand times the Indian services giants.
  • Floors are built only where a wall cannot be rented around, and China’s walls — import substitution, export controls, a billion users — were imposed upon it rather than chosen.
  • Floors survive only where a neutral institution takes custody of them, and India has no such institution, which is why the foundational work it does produce either leaves the country or stays trapped inside one firm.
  • China holds the TPC-C database world record twice over with two home-grown engines, and placed the systems software surrounding them in the Apache and CNCF commons.
  • India’s rare foundational work fits five shapes — the lone builder, the re-domiciled firm, the tool trapped in one firm, the embryonic state project, and the protocol with no floor beneath it — and none becomes a load-bearing global primitive.
  • The correction follows from the mechanism rather than from sentiment: find the wall India cannot rent around, which is the Indic-language layer and the industrial clusters, and not the elite-institute model, which faces no wall at all.
  • Across fifteen of the most load-bearing open-source projects in computing, not one foundational primitive was originated by an Indian entity; where Indians appear, as with Kafka and LLVM, they appear as co-creators inside foreign organisations that hold the asset.
  • India has built the neutral custody institution before — SITARC, for engineering metrology, in 1987 — and has never once built its software equivalent.
  • The single highest-probability target is the transaction ledger beneath UPI, because it is the one place where a world-record captive problem sits directly on top of an existing neutral custodian.
  • The claim is falsifiable: a 2030 scorecard sets concrete pass-or-fail checks, and the floor is already being poured again one layer up, in the model weights and the tokeniser.

The Paradox

Take the inputs on their own terms. India has the largest developer population on earth, the largest open-source contributor base by headcount, a software industry earning US$283bn a year, and several million new developers joining every year. Any model of how foundational software comes into existence would predict, from those inputs alone, a mature ecosystem of Indian-owned systems software: databases, brokers, runtimes, kernels. The prediction fails. The count of globally adopted foundational software projects originated by an Indian entity is not small. It is zero.

This essay is an attempt to resolve that mismatch honestly. The resolution is not that the inputs are fake — they are real, and the first section concedes them at full strength. It is that inputs were never the operative variable. China stood at a comparable point a generation ago with worse inputs on several axes and produced the floor anyway; Germany and Russia did so by two further routes. What separates them from India is not the size of the developer base but whether a wall existed that could not be rented around, and whether an institution existed to take custody of what got built.

India's Software Industry Is a Success

By every headline measure the Indian software industry is a success. It earns US$283bn in annual revenue, grew 5.1% in a weak global market, employs 5.80mn people, and now hosts the largest and fastest-growing base of developers in the world.12 The case this essay makes is not that the success is illusory. It is that the success sits in the wrong part of the stack, and that the metrics which look most impressive measure volume rather than ownership.

India Now Has the World's Largest Open-Source Contributor Base

In 2025 India added 5.2mn developers to GitHub, roughly one in seven of the 36mn new accounts worldwide and the largest intake of any country, and its public and open-source contributor base overtook that of the United States for the first time.3 India now hosts more distinct public repositories than the United States, 405k against 342k, and is projected to reach 57.5mn developers by 2030.4

Octoverse also counts public activity only, and 81.5% of all contributions on GitHub occur in private repositories, so the visible figures understate how much work happens behind closed doors.5

Indian IT Is a US$283bn Industry, Built on Exports

The industry's revenue is overwhelmingly an export of labour. Of the US$283bn in FY25 revenue, US$224bn — 79% — came from exports, against a domestic market of US$58.2bn.6 Half of that export revenue, 50.3%, is earned in the United States, with a further 31.4% in Europe and 15.5% in the United Kingdom.7

India supplies roughly 55% of the world's outsourced IT delivery, the largest single share of any country, yet it holds only about 5.5% of the total global IT-services market.89 The gap between those two figures is the argument in miniature: India is the market's supplier, not the market.

India Sells Services and Applications, Not Products It Owns

The scale established above sits in the low-capture part of the value chain. India writes and operates software that other firms own, priced below what those owners would pay to have it built at home. What India sells, in other words, is the cost gap rather than the software. That position is not an accident of temperament. A firm builds a floor only when it meets a wall it cannot rent around, and it keeps the floor only when some institution other than itself takes custody of it; India's software economy was constructed so that the first condition almost never arises and the second has no address.

The size of that gap is easiest to see in developer salaries. Built for Export; East Asia Built for Home First

India does produce genuine software products, but the successful ones were built for foreign customers from the first line of code, because the domestic market does not pay enough for Indian software. East Asia inverted that sequence: its champions were built for a captive home market and only later exported. Messaging: the Same Split, One Layer Down

Consumer messaging is not a software-vendor category, but it is the clearest test of the foundational question, because building a messenger can yield either a protocol the world adopts or merely an application at scale.

The pattern is a claim about why consumer software earns adoption at scale. It is earned in one of two ways: by building something fundamentally new that others must then build upon, which is to say a protocol, or by engineering exceptionally well upon open, peer-reviewed foundations. Signal took the first path, and WhatsApp, Google RCS, and others now encrypt with the protocol it published rather than reinvent it. WhatsApp took the second, pouring none of its own floor but standing on the best open foundations available — Erlang and its BEAM runtime, the Mnesia database, FreeBSD, an ejabberd-derived server, and Signal's cryptography — and engineering brilliantly upon them. Telegram took the first path more aggressively, building MTProto itself, and is criticised precisely because it rolled its own cryptography rather than compose vetted parts. The common thread is exposure rather than secrecy: a protocol succeeds only by publication and peer review, and great engineering on open foundations succeeds only by improving software a competitor is free to inspect and fork. India is absent from both routes. Its entrants reached the application layer, added nothing back to the foundations they consumed, and folded.

The ladder has three rungs. Firms that own foundational software are valued in the tens of millions of dollars per employee; firms that own application software, in a few million; firms that sell services, in less than a quarter of a million. The decisive observation is that Accenture, the premium Western integrator, sits in the same band as the Indian services firms rather than above them. The divider is therefore not geography or prestige but whether a firm owns a layer that others depend upon, and India's largest software firms occupy the lowest rung.

The Honest Exceptions

India has produced real foundational-adjacent software, and an honest argument must name it. Across this essay it falls into five recurring shapes, and naming the shape is itself the argument: the lone builder, an unaffiliated individual who rebuilds a tool judged wrong at the root; the re-domiciled firm, genuine floor-level work that had to become American to raise capital; the tool trapped in one firm, a real primitive built inside a single company and adopted nowhere else; the embryonic state project, institute- or state-funded, early, and not yet load-bearing; and the protocol with no floor, where India built a rail or a standard but not the systems software beneath it. The exceptions catalogued below are the first three; the embryonic state project appears with Sarvam and ShaktiDB, and the protocol-only shape with the India Stack. None of the five, so far, becomes a systems primitive the world comes to depend upon.

Kitty — the lone systems build

Kitty is a GPU-accelerated terminal emulator written by Kovid Goyal, the same independent developer in Mumbai who wrote Calibre. It is genuinely systems-tier: it offloads glyph rendering to the GPU and defines its own terminal graphics and keyboard protocols, which other tools now target. It is the cleanest available proof that the lone-obsessive archetype behind SQLite, Redis, and Nginx exists in India. It is also unaffiliated, unfunded, and singular — one person, not an industry.

Hasura — the floor India built, then re-domiciled

Hasura is the strongest foundational-adjacent company India has produced. It is a real query engine: an incoming GraphQL query is parsed into an intermediate representation, compiled to SQL, and executed with permissions enforced at the query layer, a compiler pipeline written in Haskell that other applications depend upon. Its founders are Indian and its engineering is in Bengaluru.

It is also incorporated in San Francisco and raised roughly US$136.5mn in United States venture capital, with the value and ownership sitting in America while the engineering remains in Bengaluru.[fn:hasura] India’s best foundational-adjacent company had to become American to scale. This does not refute the thesis; it is the thesis compressed into a single company.

Zerodha — open-sourced from inside a regulated broker

Zerodha open-sources the infrastructure it builds for itself — kaf-relay for Kafka replication, DungBeetle for asynchronous SQL reporting — from inside the largest Indian retail broker, a heavily regulated environment and exactly where a domestic foundational tool ought to earn trust.[fn:zerodhafin] [Author judgement] In the author’s own work on Indian central-bank digital-currency and BFSI systems, Kafka replication was a recurring pain point that kaf-relay directly addresses, yet adoption at the architectural layer met resistance. The barrier reads as trust rather than capability: an Indian-origin tool, however proven, is treated as a risk rather than a credential — the same reflex that leads the sector to buy supported foreign distributions of open source it could run directly.

The barrier appears to be trust rather than capability (Hypothesis): an Indian-origin tool, however proven, is treated as a risk rather than a credential — the same reflex that leads the sector to buy supported foreign distributions over domestic open source, discussed further below.

The reflex at the wall is the tell. When Zepto outgrew its database it switched vendors, moving from Aurora to DynamoDB to MongoDB and filing patches upstream; when Alibaba outgrew Oracle it wrote OceanBase.42 The capability was comparable and the response was opposite, because nothing forced the Indian firm to build the primitive and its economics argued for renting one.

What India Builds, Precisely

Having located India's output in the value chain, it is worth examining precisely what its most celebrated new companies and projects actually build.

The Unicorn Factory Is Mostly Non-Software Businesses

Most of India's billion-dollar "technology" companies are not software companies at all. They are conventional businesses — lenders, retailers, transporters, gaming operators — that use software as a channel to reach a customer.43 The engineering inside them is often genuine, but it is operational engineering on rented foundations rather than foundational software offered to the world.

Contribution Does Not Scale With Headcount

India's contribution to the world's shared code does not track its headcount. In 2025, contributions to open-source projects based in the European Union came to more than 934k from the United States, 553k from the United Kingdom, and 347k from India — so India, with several times the United Kingdom's developer count, contributed less in absolute terms.44

Two structural facts explain the gap. Contribution to the foundational projects themselves is tracked by employer rather than nationality: the Linux Foundation ranks Intel, Google, Oracle, Red Hat, Meta, and Huawei, and most Indian-origin systems work is done inside foreign firms and counted as theirs.45 And GitHub systematically undercounts China, because a large share of Chinese open source lives on the domestic forge Gitee.46 At the level of individuals, the one peer-reviewed measure puts India at about 5.4% of active open-source contributors, below the level its income would predict.47

The India Stack: Protocols, Not Systems Software

India leads the world in civic digital infrastructure, and it is important to concede the point plainly before qualifying it. UPI processes on the order of ₹314 lakh crore a year and accounts for roughly half of the world's real-time payments; Aadhaar has enrolled some 1.3bn people; MOSIP offers a genuinely open-source reference implementation of that identity model to other governments.525354 These are protocols and public rails, and they are real. They are not, however, the systems floor — the databases, runtimes, and message brokers on which they themselves run, much of it foreign and hosted on foreign clouds. India's civic infrastructure is world-class at the protocol and application layers, and it does not extend to the layer beneath.

What Software Actually Is

The argument rests on a distinction between three layers of software, which it is worth stating precisely.

Layer What it is Examples
Application what a person uses wallets, dashboards, office suites
Middleware glue between applications and the floor frameworks, query engines, gateways
Foundational (systems) the floor everything runs on operating systems, databases, message brokers, runtimes, cryptography

The trade term for the bottom row is systems software. Other software depends upon it, and when it fails everything above it fails at once. Value follows that dependency, because whoever owns the floor sets the price, the roadmap, the licence, and the shutdown date for everyone standing on it. A nation strong only in the top row is a tenant, however prosperous.

Read the diagram twice. Downward, it is a dependency graph: nothing in the top row executes without every box in the bottom row. Upward, it is a rent ledger.

Renting the Floor — the Empty Middle

Before the evidence, the mechanism it is evidence for. Two conditions must hold together. A wall the builder cannot rent around forces the floor into existence; an institution independent of the builder keeps it in existence and lets others adopt it. Remove either and the outcome is not a floor but one of four familiar failures, each of which has an Indian name.

The mechanism has a second half that is easy to miss, because it operates at the level of the industry rather than the firm. For any single company the decision to rent is usually correct. The question a firm actually asks is narrow — do we need this capability, is it cheaper to build than to rent, and do we need long-term strategic independence from whoever owns it — and for almost every Indian firm the honest answer at the third gate is no, because the horizon that would justify building is longer than the horizon on which the firm is judged.

Same Start, Different Choice

A generation ago China stood roughly where India stands now, assembling and servicing what others had designed. Ranked on software around the year 2000, India was ahead, on the strength of English, its services contracts, and an export head start. What changed was not the starting line but the direction each country pointed its industry, and India's own attempts at a floor show that the difference was one of direction rather than capacity.

BOSS — the announcement mistaken for the work

BOSS, or Bharat Operating System Solutions, is a Debian derivative from C-DAC that has been promoted since 2007 as India’s national Linux. It never achieved adoption at scale, even within government. Re-skinning Debian is configuring an operating system, not building one; the floor remained foreign, and only the wallpaper was Indian.

Kerala — the forcing function that worked by accident

Kerala placed free software in its schools from the mid-2000s, through the programme now known as KITE, because it could not afford proprietary licences. Two decades on, the state produces a disproportionate share of India’s Malayalam-language and free-software contributors. The forcing function was poverty, as it usually is.

China Built the Floor

China's move into foundational software was driven by strategy and by siege: the policy of removing IBM servers, Oracle databases, and EMC storage from critical systems; export controls; and consumer platforms operating at a scale that purchased Western software could not survive.

The asymmetry deserves stating plainly, because it is what makes these forcing functions rather than resolutions. China did not select its walls. Import substitution was a response to a foreign dependency judged unsafe, export controls were imposed from outside, and a billion simultaneous users are a fact of demography. A constraint one does not face cannot be adopted by decision, which is why the instruction to India is not to emulate China's resolve but to find the wall it already cannot rent around.

The story of OceanBase

In 2010 Alipay ran on Oracle, and on Singles’ Day, the largest shopping event on earth, the load exceeded what any purchased database was built to survive. Ant Group concluded that the database itself was wrong for the problem and rebuilt it, in C++, from the basics, over the better part of a decade.

It replaced Oracle in production gradually, first for a tenth of Alipay’s traffic and then for all of it. In October 2019 OceanBase recorded 60.88mn tpmC on the TPC-C benchmark, taking the record from Oracle’s 2010 mark of 30.25mn; in May 2020 it reached 707mn, a result later published at VLDB; and by 2025 Alibaba Cloud’s PolarDB had pushed the record to 2.055bn.[fn:oceanbase][fn:tpcc] A firm that rented Oracle a decade earlier came to hold the record Oracle once owned.

The lesson is not that Chinese engineers are uniquely gifted. It is that a firm which had the right building blocks made the difficult and riskier choice to build a foundation rather than buy around the wall. One Western brick even remains: OceanBase’s stored-procedure compiler is built on LLVM. Nobody pours every floor; the point is that China chose to pour most of it.

The same reflex recurred across other firms. ByteDance built the CloudWeGo microservice frameworks, in Go and Rust, for TikTok-scale traffic. Huawei shipped the openEuler operating system and the openGauss database when RHEL became a strategic risk. PingCAP built TiDB, a distributed SQL database that implements the design in Google's Spanner paper as open source. Between 2015 and 2024, fourteen infrastructure projects of Chinese origin were donated to the Apache Software Foundation and now run in production worldwide.66

The story of Gitee

Not all of it came from giants under siege. Gitee began as a domestic Git-hosting forge and became China’s answer to GitHub — today the state-backed default where a large share of Chinese open source lives, which is precisely why GitHub-based measures undercount China. China did not only build its floor through state-mandated champions; it also repatriated the developer infrastructure itself, an option India has never taken up even though it operates DigiLocker and a national document-verification system embedded in the school curriculum, the largest captive market imaginable, to little adoption.

Products vs Services, and Who Contributes the Floor

At the platform layer — the software that runs private clouds and telecom cores — the difference between the two countries is not capability but posture: China contributes to the source, and India ships or buys the binaries.

In private cloud, the Chinese firm 99Cloud has been a top-ten global contributor to OpenStack, while the Indian firm StackUp ships an OpenStack-based product with little upstream contribution and few large deployments.67 In the telecom core, both countries built a sovereign stack, which isolates the difference to openness. China Telecom, a state-owned operator, ran an OpenStack-Powered cloud and co-founded the Open-O project that merged into ONAP, now a global orchestration standard; BSNL's Bharat stack, built by C-DOT, Tejas, and TCS, is indigenous but closed and delivered by a systems integrator.6869

Author judgement — why the floor stays foreign in India

India’s most software-influential sector is banking, and its largest buyers, the public and private banks under RBI oversight, take a no-risk posture even in pilots. They consume open source only through commercial, supported distributions: RHEL rather than upstream Linux, OpenShift rather than upstream Kubernetes, never Nomad, and never a domestic newcomer such as StackUp. Several are publicly celebrated for “open-source excellence” while running exclusively Red Hat’s paid, de-risked wrapper of that open source.[fn:redhatbanks] China’s policy excludes foreign platforms and thereby creates captive demand; India’s local-content rules reward badging and are defeated at the point of purchase by risk aversion.

Open-source contribution is itself a trust signal, a public proof of competence that no sales deck can fake. StackUp contributes little upstream, so a risk-averse buyer has no such proof and defaults to the vendor that has spent a decade advertising exactly that. Were StackUp to contribute materially to OpenStack, Indian buyers would likely take it more seriously — not because the code would be better, but because the credential would exist (Hypothesis). The absence is self-reinforcing: no contribution, no credibility, no sales, and no resources with which to contribute.

Comparators: Germany and Russia

China is not the only country that built a floor, and it is not even the most transferable example, because its delete-everything mandate is peculiar to its politics. Two other cases show that foundations can be built under quite different forcing functions, which matters for an India looking for a route it can actually take.

Germany built foundations through sovereignty rather than siege. SUSE, one of the oldest commercial Linux distributions, is German;70 LibreOffice is stewarded by The Document Foundation, a German Stiftung;71 and Nextcloud, a German company, runs Deutsche Telekom's MagentaCLOUD at 2.2 million users and 7.2 PB under GDPR.72 The forcing function is procurement for digital sovereignty: the state of Schleswig-Holstein is migrating tens of thousands of public workstations from Windows and Microsoft Office to Linux and LibreOffice, explicitly to control its own data, with Fraunhofer institutes and industry foundations backing the wider effort.73 The governance model is as important as the software, because a foundation held by a neutral Stiftung outlives the firm that began it. This is the most transferable model to India: procurement for sovereignty, a neutral custodian, and a slow rather than explosive build.

Russia built foundations organically, and then ran into the limits of geopolitics. Nginx was written by Igor Sysoev at Rambler and became one of the most widely deployed web servers in the world, though it is now owned by the American company F5.74 ClickHouse, the column-store database, originated at Yandex before spinning out as an independent company that has since relocated. Maintenance, moreover, is a multi-decade undertaking rather than a launch: Juergen Gross at SUSE still keeps the kernel's Xen and x86 paths alive upstream, a reminder that custody is a standing obligation, not a one-time act.75 The Russian case is a caution as much as a model: organic invention built real foundations, but ownership and custody migrated once capital and geopolitics intervened.

How Foundations Get Built

Across the most load-bearing pieces of systems software, five development patterns recur. It is worth stating them first and then testing them against the historical record, because the patterns, not the individual stories, are what India must learn to reproduce.

# Pattern China instance India instance
1 a lone obsessive rebuilds a tool judged wrong at the root rare, usually firm-backed Goyal, with Calibre and Kitty, unaffiliated
2 a mass community forms with no single owner growing, via the Gitee ecosystem thin
3 an institution commissions a named deliverable for a real user the delete-IOE mandate produced OceanBase mis-aimed at services export and defence hardware
4 a specialist builds the tool their own field lacks present rare
5 a scale problem refuses every workaround Alipay's scale produced OceanBase and RocketMQ present, but buyers rent around it

The historical record bears the patterns out. SQLite began with a single engineer on a defence contract before entering the public domain; Linux began as a student's hobby a community adopted; Git and Nginx and Redis each began with one person meeting a wall that would not yield; PostgreSQL and Kubernetes began as institutional projects handed to a community; the Web and the R language were built by specialists who needed a tool their field lacked; and OceanBase and Hadoop were forced into being by scale.76 Two sources of foundations are conspicuously absent from that list: the elite teaching institution, and the state programme aimed at a captive in-house user. Those are the two sources on which India has leaned hardest.

India's state laboratories are the clearest case of the third pattern mis-aimed. C-DAC, C-DOT, ISRO, BARC, and DRDO produce substantial software, but it is built for captive institutional use, rarely open-sourced, and rarely adopted outside the sponsoring agency. The navigation programme is the sharp example: India built NavIC, the constellation and the signal, but NavIC reaches consumer devices only through foreign silicon, having been added by Qualcomm, MediaTek, and Broadcom to their own chips, while India's own navigation chips remain at the research and space tier. India's newest indigenous processor, C-DAC's DHRUV64 — a 64-bit dual-core RISC-V part released under the national microprocessor programme late in 2025 — sits at that same tier, a test chip at the silicon floor rather than a consumer product, and, despite occasional loose reporting, a processor and not an operating system — an application-class part that runs Linux, taped out on a 28nm node abroad.77 India builds the system and the protocol; it does not build, and does not get adopted at, the silicon and systems-software floor.

The supercomputing programme shows the same pattern misfiring at the level of a single component, and it is the sharpest case because here the entity was Indian. C-DAC did not merely assemble PARAM machines from imported parts. It designed PARAMNet-3 around its own GEMINI network co-processor — a device that offloaded the transport layer into hardware with direct user-level access to endpoints, supporting send/receive, RDMA, and datagram services over PCI Express at 10 Gbps, feeding a 48-port switch at sub-microsecond latency — and it wrote the accompanying software stack, Kshipra, described in C-DAC's own annual report as a complete program development environment, with the work completed across 2007-08.78 This was precisely the class of device whose drivers live upstream in the Linux kernel's InfiniBand and RDMA subsystems, where Mellanox, Chelsio, Myricom, and Quadrics all maintained mainline support in the same period. [Inferred from absence] No PARAMNet, GEMINI, or Kshipra driver appears in the mainline kernel. The hardware was retired, the production Supercomputing Mission systems standardised on Mellanox InfiniBand with Mellanox's upstream driver, and nothing of the Indian effort survives in the shared tree.

The lesson is narrower and harder than the usual one. India built the interconnect, built the stack to drive it, and kept both inside the institution — then abandoned the hardware and rented someone else's, whose driver anyone in the world can read, extend, and depend upon. An Indian entity owned this work outright, which is the condition the rest of this essay says is missing, and it still accrued to nothing, because ownership without publication is a drawer rather than a floor. Entity nationality is necessary; it is not sufficient. The asset has to be held in the open for anyone, including India, to build on it later.

The Kernel Test

Most of this essay argues about layers that are hard to audit from outside. Who really owns a database, who maintains a broker, whose engineers carry a project — these are matters of judgement, and a determined sceptic can contest each one. The Linux kernel removes the difficulty. It is roughly forty million lines, about sixty percent of them device drivers, and every line is signed, dated, and attributed in a public git history.79 It is the one place in software where the question who built the floor has a machine-checkable answer.

Run the query for India and the answer is stark. No India-headquartered vendor maintains a driver family in the mainline kernel, and no core kernel subsystem — no filesystem, no scheduler, no subsystem of record — originates with an Indian organisation. [Inferred from absence] Indian engineers are abundantly present in the kernel; they are present as employees of Intel, Qualcomm, Red Hat, IBM, and Google, writing those companies' code under those companies' copyright.

What an Entity Contribution Looks Like

China supplies the control case, and it does not merely contribute drivers — it originates core kernel technology. EROFS, the Enhanced Read-Only File System, was written by Gao Xiang at Huawei, entered kernel staging in 4.14 in 2018, was merged to the mainline in Linux 5.4 in 2019, and is now the default read-only filesystem for Android itself, mandated by Google for Android 13's read-only partitions and shipped by Oppo, Xiaomi, and Samsung; its reviewers now sit at Huawei, Alibaba, Vivo, and Google.80 A Chinese firm authored a filesystem that the entire Android world runs on, and Huawei's name is on it in perpetuity, in a tree no one can take away from it. Across the 5.x series Huawei climbed to the top of the per-release contributor tables by changeset count, reaching first place at 5.8 and again at 5.10.81

That is what an entity contribution looks like: the work is done, the credit is permanent, the maintainership is held, and the capability compounds inside an organisation that is not going anywhere.

The Indian Case, Stated Precisely

The instinctive Indian rebuttal is that Indians do contribute to the kernel, and it is correct. The rebuttal does not survive contact with the question of which entity.

The clearest instance is kprobes, the kernel's dynamic instrumentation facility, whose base patch was submitted in August 2004 by Prasanna S Panchamukhi of the IBM Linux Technology Center, India Software Labs, Bangalore, and which merged to the mainline in 2.6.9 that November.82 Kprobes is genuine core infrastructure — every tracing and observability tool in the Linux world stands on it two decades later. It was originated by an India-based team, at an Indian address, staffed by Indian engineers.

It is IBM's. The copyright is IBM's, the maintainership sits within IBM's structure, and the institutional memory of how and why it was built lives in Armonk rather than Bangalore. The same is true of the ongoing work from that lab — tracing, memory management, scheduler and cgroup bandwidth control — and of the India-based maintainers at other foreign firms, such as the dmaengine subsystem maintained from India under Linaro's umbrella. If IBM were to close its Bangalore laboratory tomorrow, the code would remain in the kernel under IBM's name and nothing of the capability would remain in India as an institution.

This is the essay's central distinction reduced to its cleanest form. Skill was present. Location was Indian. The entity was not, and so nothing accrued. Kprobes is not a counter-example to the thesis; it is the exhibit that demonstrates it.

A foundation survives its originator only if some neutral body will take custody of it — the Apache Software Foundation, the CNCF, a German Stiftung. This is the difference between MOSIP, which a neutral international setting carried to other governments, and Drove, which remains excellent and alone in one firm's repository. India's failures here are of aim rather than capacity: it built forcing functions and pointed them at services export, and it built institutions and pointed them at defence hardware.

India's Handful

Where the conditions did align, India produced real foundational software, and the scarcity of the examples is itself the evidence. The measure that matters is adoption, the reach that separates a floor from a private tool.

Project What it does Adoption

Frappe / ERPNext a metadata-driven web framework in Python and MariaDB, on which ERPNext is built a community in the tens of thousands; runs Zerodha's back office91

FLOSS/fund — India funds the floor it did not build

FLOSS/fund is not a product but a funding mechanism: Zerodha commits US$1mn a year to critical open-source projects worldwide. Its 2025 grantees include OpenSSL, FFmpeg, Blender, OpenStreetMap, Krita, and Weblate, alongside US$500k to the GitHub Secure Open Source Fund and roughly US$100k to FOSS United.[fn:flossfund] The grantee list is the point. India’s most significant open-source institutional act funds foundations built elsewhere — OpenSSL, FFmpeg, Blender — rather than any built at home. It is a tenant paying, generously, to maintain the building, and it is the clearest single illustration of the thesis.

Author judgement — ShaktiDB is BOSS, one layer down

ShaktiDB, also branded BharatDB, is India’s first entry in the database row, and it is instructive precisely because of what it is: a customised fork of community PostgreSQL, pinned at version 17.4, funded by a government grant, developed by an elite institute’s innovation hub together with a national computing centre, and taken to government buyers by a systems integrator. That is the exact configuration described elsewhere in this essay — a reskin of a foreign foundation rather than a new engine, delivered into captive institutional demand — and it is the database-floor analogue of BOSS, the Debian re-skin of the previous decade.

The tell is the posture. The project is presented as open and sovereign, yet it ships as a closed fork that has already fallen behind its upstream, and the contribution that would actually build a floor — open-sourcing the compliance and security patches so that every Indian vendor could build on them — is the one it has not made. Whether ShaktiDB escapes the captive, integrator-delivered fate of BOSS and the BSNL stack is the single cleanest near-term test of this essay’s claim, which is why it appears on the scorecard below.

MOSIP reached the world because its forcing function arrived with a real external user, a government that had no identity system, which pulled the software into the open. That is the condition Drove lacked. India can build the floor; what it has never built is the institution that keeps a floor and makes it spread.

The firms that behave most like technology companies are, tellingly, the ones that produce this rare work. PhonePe repatriated its workloads from public cloud to its own data centres and built Drove; Zerodha deliberately chose HashiCorp's Nomad over Kubernetes, open-sourced the glue around it, and runs ERPNext for its own back office. Zerodha's economics make the point concrete: on the order of ₹8,000 crore of revenue against roughly 1,150 employees, it earns close to US$870k per employee, an order of magnitude above the Indian services giants, which is a technology company's leverage rather than a broker's. The capability tracks the technology-company mindset; even so, what it produces is glue, or a primitive trapped inside one firm.

The Turn

Elite Institution, Government Grant, and Industry Partnership Have Not Worked

India's theory of technological progress — concentrate the ablest in a few elite institutions, add government grants and a partnership with an industry leader, and expect breakthroughs — is aimed against the mechanism that actually produces foundations. Elite institutions optimise for individual exit: the graduate who could build a database has the best exit options in the country, and the trajectory points outward, or upward into the application layer, rather than toward the patient and unpaid work of a floor built at home. Meanwhile the services firms that were meant to anchor the industry are precisely what artificial intelligence now marks down. The top-down model is losing both of its engines at once. ShaktiDB (above) is this same configuration observed in real time, and the scorecard below treats its trajectory as the cleanest near-term test of whether the pattern still holds.

Knowledge Is Democratised; the Opening Is Bottom-Up

The knowledge required to build foundational software — the papers, the reference implementations, the model weights, the tooling — is now largely open, and the gate the elite institutions once kept has fallen. The decisive question is no longer who holds the knowledge but who faces a real problem with no budget to evade it, which is the forcing function itself.

Model Forcing function Builders Governance
elite institute and large firm weak; no real wall exit-bound grant-based
industrial cluster and local polytechnic strong; a real problem, no budget locally rooted open by default

Kerala produced a version of this outcome by accident, as noted above, when a lack of licensing money put free software into its schools and produced a contributor base out of proportion to the state's size.

The One Problem That Is India's Alone

The sharpest target is the problem that artificial intelligence is now rewriting and that is structurally India's to solve: language. India has twenty-two official languages and about a dozen scripts, and a billion people whom the current models serve badly. General-purpose tokenisers fragment Indic scripts so inefficiently that every Indian model runs slower and costs more to train and serve, a penalty captured in the token-fertility figures of India's own model efforts.94 This is the wall India cannot rent around, and by the mechanism set out at the start of this essay that is precisely why a floor could be forced into existence here and almost nowhere else in the Indian economy. No American or Chinese firm will fix this properly, because the pain is not theirs; the forcing function is real, domestic, and un-outsourceable, which is the exact condition that produced foundations everywhere else in this essay. The pieces already exist, scattered across AI4Bharat, Sarvam, and Bhashini,95 and what is missing is the decision to consolidate them into a layer beneath every Indian model.

The problem reaches below the model, into the script itself. The keyboard is a global standard in English, and Indic input has no comparable settled hardware; even the rendering of Indian scripts on screen runs on HarfBuzz, the text-shaping engine built substantially abroad. The layer that draws India's own letters was poured elsewhere, which is the essay's argument reduced to a single, uncomfortable example.

The One Bet Worth Making

Everything to this point has been diagnostic. This section is not. If India were to make a single concentrated attempt at foundational software — one target, chosen for the highest probability of succeeding rather than the highest prestige — the answer is not an operating system, not a language, and not a chip. It is the transaction ledger beneath UPI, built in the open, held by NPCI.

The case is not enthusiasm. It is that this target, alone among the candidates, already satisfies every condition this essay has argued to be necessary, and satisfies them without anyone having to manufacture them first.

The Condition Nobody Can Manufacture Is Already Met

A foundational primitive requires a wall that cannot be rented around. Almost every proposed Indian initiative fails at exactly this point: the problem is real, but a vendor will sell an adequate answer, so the forcing function never bites. UPI is the exception. In December 2025 the system processed 21.63 billion transactions worth about ₹27.97 lakh crore, running near 698 million transactions a day; across FY 2025-26 it handled on the order of 241 billion transactions against roughly ₹314 lakh crore in value.96 That is the largest real-time retail payment system in the world by transaction count, by a wide margin.

At that volume the economics invert. Commercial database licensing stops being a line item and becomes a structural cost of the national payment rail, and failure modes stop being operational and become political — the intermittent transaction declines of late March 2025 were front-page news rather than an incident ticket.97 [Author judgement] This is the precise condition under which organisations stop shopping and start building, because nothing on the shelf fits and no price improves the fit.

It Is the Path China Actually Took

The mechanism is already established in this essay. Alipay's scale refused every workaround and OceanBase came out of it — a distributed database that went on to take the TPC-C world record and seed an entire national database sector. That was not industrial policy in the abstract. It was one payments system at unprecedented scale needing a ledger that did not exist.

India has the payments system. It does not have the ledger. It runs the world's largest real-time payment network on database and messaging software it does not own, did not write, and cannot modify. The structural parallel is exact, and it is the strongest available evidence that the target is achievable rather than aspirational: the identical problem, at comparable scale, produced a foundational database once already, in a country that started further behind.

The Custody Institution Already Exists

This essay's central claim is that India's deficit is custody rather than capability. The reason this target ranks first is that the custody institution is already built, already neutral, and already sitting at the right point in the stack.

NPCI was established in 2008 as a not-for-profit company, an initiative of the Reserve Bank of India and the Indian Banks' Association under the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007, owned by a consortium of banks rather than by any single institution.98 [Author judgement] Structurally this is the SITARC model at national scale, and it is the same shape as an Apache or a CNCF: a member-owned body holding shared infrastructure that no participant owns individually. Every other proposal in this space must invent its custodian and then hope the institution outlives its founders. Here the institution exists, has operated for nearly two decades, already holds a piece of national infrastructure, and has the standing to hold a second.

The builders are not exit-bound either. The engineering runs across NPCI, the member banks — 694 of them live on UPI as of early 2025 — and the payment-service layer around them.99 These are institutions with a permanent stake in the rail continuing to work, which is the closest thing to a patient builder the Indian software economy has.

Adoption Is the Hardest Condition, and It Is Solved

What kills most foundational projects is not construction but adoption. Something gets built, nobody outside the sponsoring organisation runs it, and it dies as a captive artefact. That is precisely how BOSS and the state-laboratory software failed, and it is the risk hanging over ShaktiDB now. A ledger built for UPI does not have that problem, because UPI itself is being exported: the protocol is going out to other jurisdictions, and infrastructure that ships with it goes out too. A primitive with a live domestic user at world-record scale and a ready international distribution path has already cleared the bar that stops almost everything else in this essay.

What Precisely Should Be Built

Not "a database." The target is narrow and nameable: a horizontally scalable, strictly consistent transaction ledger and settlement engine, tuned for very high volumes of very small payments, developed in the open under a permissive licence, with copyright and trademark held by NPCI or by a foundation NPCI establishes for the purpose. Around it, the adjacent primitives the same workload forces into existence — a durable message log for settlement events, a reconciliation engine, a schema and wire format for inter-bank instruction. This is the same cluster of components that grew around OceanBase, and for the same reason: one workload, hard enough, generates a family of primitives rather than a single artefact.

The governance requirement is not decorative, and it is the part most likely to be got wrong, because the PARAMNet case shows what happens when it is. The work must be open from the first commit rather than open-sourced on completion; contribution must be possible from outside the member banks; and custody must sit with the institution rather than with whichever systems integrator is engaged to build it. Each of those three has failed before in Indian practice, which is why they are stated as conditions rather than assumed as defaults.

The Honest Objection

The strongest argument against this target is not technical and deserves stating plainly rather than handling. NPCI is a regulated systemic utility whose first obligation is availability. Utilities under an availability mandate are correctly risk-averse, and a live national payment rail is the worst imaginable place to experiment with unproven infrastructure. The culture that keeps UPI running is close to the opposite of the culture that builds new systems software.

[Author judgement] The answer is that OceanBase did not begin by replacing Alipay's database either. It was built beside the production system, proved at shadow volume against real traffic for years, and migrated workload by workload once it had earned the right. That path is available here, and it is the only responsible version of this proposal: build alongside, prove at scale without carrying it, migrate incrementally, and let the production obligation serve as the quality bar rather than as the reason not to start.

Why Not the Alternatives

The other candidates are worth building and are weaker as the single concentrated bet. Indic-language computing has the most defensible captive problem — no one else will solve it — but its output is mostly models and application tooling rather than systems software, and its adoption path is largely domestic. The cluster primitives of the next section have the right mechanism and the right builders, but operate at a scale where adoption spreads slowly. AI infrastructure has no forcing function at all while the compute itself is rented. None of these has a captive problem at world-record scale sitting directly on top of an existing neutral custodian. That combination exists exactly once.

Foundational Targets, Mapped to Industrial Clusters

The pattern that builds foundations is a real problem, no budget with which to evade it, and builders who are not on their way out. India's lesser-known industrial clusters supply exactly that combination, each pairing a captive problem with a nearby technical institution.

It is the same four conditions, assembled in the places the elite model never looks.

The Cluster Is Already a System — Coimbatore

The reason to name clusters rather than institutes is that a working cluster is not a list of firms; it is a network with a shared substrate, and the substrate is where the leverage sits. Coimbatore is the clearest instance. Its visible face is a directory — pump makers, foundries in Ganapathy and Kanuvai, textile machinery, auto components — but the structure underneath is a dependency graph. Raw material and casting feed a machining-and-tooling layer; that layer feeds component suppliers, who feed module suppliers, who feed OEMs selling into domestic and export markets. The same tier logic that maps Chennai or Pune applies here and is almost never drawn for Coimbatore specifically.

The structural point is horizontal rather than vertical. A single machining shop quotes a pump manufacturer, an auto-component OEM, and a medical-device firm in the same week; the capability is owned by no one industry and shared across all of them, which is exactly why the cluster is resilient — when textile-machinery demand softens, the same capacity turns to automotive or renewables. [Author judgement] This is foundational-layer logic expressed in hardware. Value and durability attach to the shared capability that many verticals depend upon, not to any single vertical standing on it, which is the same relation systems software has to the applications above it. A cluster is a floor with tenants.

What makes Coimbatore load-bearing for this argument is that the cluster built its own custody institution. SITARC — the Scientific and Industrial Testing and Research Centre, established in 1987 — was set up by the two industry associations, SIEMA and CODISSIA, as a not-for-profit registered society independent of any single firm, NABL-accredited to ISO/IEC 17025, providing testing, calibration, and metrology to engineering-industry members across India.100 Metrology is the substrate every manufacturer in the cluster depends on and none of them individually owns: the invisible common floor beneath the visible machining.

[Author judgement] SITARC is to the Coimbatore hardware cluster what a neutral foundation — Apache, CNCF, a German Stiftung — is to the software floor: the independent commons that lets an ecosystem stand on shared ground rather than on each firm's private ground. Read against the custody argument running through this essay, the point is uncomfortable and exact. India built the keep-the-floor institution — neutral, member-owned, outliving any one participant — for engineering metrology, in Coimbatore, in 1987, decades before the question was posed for software. It has never built the equivalent for systems software. The talent anchors are in place too: PSG College of Technology, Kumaraguru College of Technology, and Coimbatore Institute of Technology supply the engineering base, and machine-tool financing runs through local banks and NBFCs. Every condition the software floor lacks is present, in hardware, in one district, assembled by the cluster and held by no single firm.

The generalisable claim is therefore not that India should fund more clusters. It is that the cluster already demonstrates the mechanism the software industry never reproduced: a captive problem, exit-resistant builders, and — the piece that matters most — a neutral institution that takes custody of the shared floor. The targets in the table above are worth pursuing because that machine is not hypothetical. It has been running in Coimbatore since 1987; it has simply never been pointed at a systems-software primitive.

Conclusion

The chart that circulates every few months is not wrong about the numbers; it is wrong about what they mean. India is a large, capable, application-layer software economy that sells discounted labour. China is the deeper success, not because it has more engineers or better ones, but because it chose to own the floor, manufactured the conditions that force foundations into existence, and continued until it had a stack. Germany and Russia show that the Chinese mandate is not the only road: sovereignty procurement and organic invention build floors too, provided a neutral body takes custody of the result.

The difference was never talent, for India has supplied the world's foundational software with its people for decades, only not at home. It was direction, and direction is a choice, which means it remains available.

Software Worth Building in the Next Twenty Years

If India is to build a floor, the targets most likely to reward the effort share one trait: they are problems the rest of the world has little reason to solve on India's behalf.

  • Indic-language foundational AI, and the tokenisation layer beneath it.
  • Offline-first and intermittent-connectivity systems software.
  • Data infrastructure for agricultural and industrial clusters.
  • Systems software close to domestic silicon, as and when a fabrication base returns.
  • Sovereign developer infrastructure — a domestic forge that is actually adopted, rather than merely announced.

Twenty years is the horizon, and it is chosen advisedly, because we have all seen what became of the smart cities announced in 2014.

The floor is already being poured again, one layer up, in the model weights and the tokeniser on which the next decade's software will run, and the same five shapes are reappearing there. That is the subject of a companion essay, Renting the Model.

<div class="thesis">
<div class="story-label">Core thesis</div>
<p>
Value, durability, and strategic leverage in software accrue to whoever owns the
foundational floor — the databases, brokers, meshes, consensus systems, kernels, and
runtimes that everything else is built upon — and a nation strong only in the layer
above is a tenant, however large its developer base. Floors come into existence under
two conditions, both necessary: a wall that cannot be rented around, and an institution
independent of the builder that takes custody of the result so that it stays and
spreads. China was handed the first — import substitution, export controls, and a
billion users at once — and built the second, donating its systems software to the
Apache and CNCF commons. India has had neither: its firms could always buy their way
around every wall, rationally, and the foundational work its engineers did build has
had nowhere to live.
</p>
<p>
The sharpest form of the claim is not that India declined to build the floor. Hasura,
YugabyteDB, and MinIO are floor-level software with Indian founders at their origin, and
the custody of all three now sits in the United States. India has no institution that
keeps the floor it builds.
</p>
<p>
The unit that matters is therefore the entity, not the passport and not the office
address. A contribution builds national capability only when an Indian entity owns the
result and holds it where others can build upon it. Skill in Indian hands that accrues
to a foreign balance sheet is not a national asset; it is an export of the asset at the
moment of its creation. By that measure India's record is not thin but empty, and the
deficit it exposes is custody rather than capability.
</p>

<div class="brief">
<div class="story-label">In brief</div>
<ul>
<li>India has the world's largest developer base and the largest open-source contributor base by headcount, having overtaken the United States for the first time.</li>
<li>Its output is application-layer and built for export: US$283bn in revenue, 79% of it sold abroad, and almost no systems software owned.</li>
<li>Value in software accrues to whoever owns the foundational floor that other software depends upon.</li>
<li>Measured by market value per employee, the firms that own the floor are worth one hundred to one thousand times the Indian services giants.</li>
<li>Floors are built only where a wall cannot be rented around, and China's walls — import substitution, export controls, a billion users — were imposed upon it rather than chosen.</li>
<li>Floors survive only where a neutral institution takes custody of them, and India has no such institution, which is why the foundational work it does produce either leaves the country or stays trapped inside one firm.</li>
<li>China holds the TPC-C database world record twice over with two home-grown engines, and placed the systems software surrounding them in the Apache and CNCF commons.</li>
<li>India's rare foundational work fits five shapes — the lone builder, the re-domiciled firm, the tool trapped in one firm, the embryonic state project, and the protocol with no floor beneath it — and none becomes a load-bearing global primitive.</li>
<li>The correction follows from the mechanism rather than from sentiment: find the wall India cannot rent around, which is the Indic-language layer and the industrial clusters, and not the elite-institute model, which faces no wall at all.</li>
<li>Across fifteen of the most load-bearing open-source projects in computing, not one foundational primitive was originated by an Indian entity; where Indians appear, as with Kafka and LLVM, they appear as co-creators inside foreign organisations that hold the asset.</li>
<li>India has built the neutral custody institution before — SITARC, for engineering metrology, in 1987 — and has never once built its software equivalent.</li>
<li>The single highest-probability target is the transaction ledger beneath UPI, because it is the one place where a world-record captive problem sits directly on top of an existing neutral custodian.</li>
<li>The claim is falsifiable: a 2030 scorecard sets concrete pass-or-fail checks, and the floor is already being poured again one layer up, in the model weights and the tokeniser.</li>
</ul>
</div>

# The Paradox {#paradox}

Take the inputs on their own terms. India has the largest developer
population on earth, the largest open-source contributor base by
headcount, a software industry earning US\$283bn a year, and several
million new developers joining every year. Any model of how foundational
software comes into existence would predict, from those inputs alone, a
mature ecosystem of Indian-owned systems software: databases, brokers,
runtimes, kernels. The prediction fails. The count of globally adopted
foundational software projects originated by an Indian entity is not
small. It is zero.

resolution is not that the inputs are fake --- they are real, and the
first section concedes them at full strength. It is that inputs were
never the operative variable. China stood at a comparable point a
generation ago with worse inputs on several axes and produced the floor
anyway; Germany and Russia did so by two further routes. What separates
them from India is not the size of the developer base but whether a wall
existed that could not be rented around, and whether an institution
existed to take custody of what got built.

# India\'s Software Industry Is a Success {#scale}

By every headline measure the Indian software industry is a success. It
earns US\$283bn in annual revenue, grew 5.1% in a weak global market,
employs 5.80mn people, and now hosts the largest and fastest-growing
base of developers in the world.[^1][^2] The case this essay makes is
not that the success is illusory. It is that the success sits in the
wrong part of the stack, and that the metrics which look most impressive
measure volume rather than ownership.

## India Now Has the World\'s Largest Open-Source Contributor Base {#talent}

In 2025 India added 5.2mn developers to GitHub, roughly one in seven of
the 36mn new accounts worldwide and the largest intake of any country,
and its public and open-source contributor base overtook that of the
United States for the first time.[^3] India now hosts more distinct
public repositories than the United States, 405k against 342k, and is
projected to reach 57.5mn developers by 2030.[^4]

  Metric (GitHub Octoverse 2025)          India                  United States
  --------------------------------------- ---------------------- ---------------
  New developers added in the year        5.2mn                  ---
  Public / open-source contributor base   largest in the world   second
  Distinct public repositories            405k                   342k
  Share of total contributions            \~12.5%                \~31.8%

The qualification matters as much as the achievement. India leads on the
number of contributors but trails the United States on the volume of
contribution, because output per developer is thinner. Octoverse also
counts public activity only, and 81.5% of all contributions on GitHub
occur in private repositories, so the visible figures understate how
much work happens behind closed doors.[^5]

## Indian IT Is a US\$283bn Industry, Built on Exports {#revenue}

The industry\'s revenue is overwhelmingly an export of labour. Of the
US\$283bn in FY25 revenue, US\$224bn --- 79% --- came from exports,
against a domestic market of US\$58.2bn.[^6] Half of that export
revenue, 50.3%, is earned in the United States, with a further 31.4% in
Europe and 15.5% in the United Kingdom.[^7]

India supplies roughly 55% of the world\'s outsourced IT delivery, the
largest single share of any country, yet it holds only about 5.5% of the
total global IT-services market.[^8][^9] The gap between those two
figures is the argument in miniature: India is the market\'s supplier,
not the market.

# India Sells Services and Applications, Not Products It Owns {#value-chain}

The scale established above sits in the low-capture part of the value
chain. India writes and operates software that other firms own, priced
below what those owners would pay to have it built at home. What India
sells, in other words, is the cost gap rather than the software. That
position is not an accident of temperament. A firm builds a floor only
when it meets a wall it cannot rent around, and it keeps the floor only
when some institution other than itself takes custody of it; India\'s
software economy was constructed so that the first condition almost
never arises and the second has no address.

## The Value India Provides Is the Cost Gap {#arbitrage}

The size of that gap is easiest to see in developer salaries. A
mid-level engineer in the United States costs roughly five to fifteen
times an equivalent engineer in India, and while the two survey sources
below disagree on the point values --- one measures posted salaries, the
other loaded compensation --- they agree on the ratio, which is the
figure that matters.[^10][^11]

  Market           Gini Talent 2025   whatisthesalary 2025--26
  ---------------- ------------------ --------------------------
  United States    US\$99--111k       US\$130--150k base
  United Kingdom   US\$55k            ---
  Germany          US\$52--64k        ---
  India            US\$15--35k        US\$15--35k

A cost gap is not a moat. It narrows as wages rise, and it is already
being undercut one tier down by the Philippines and Vietnam. Arbitrage
is a position that must be defended forever and can be taken by whoever
is next cheapest.

the domestic market does not pay enough for Indian software. East Asia

sold outward and never met that wall, an argument developed in full in
the section on China below.

### What Is Excluded, and Why {#excluded}

The test for the table above is whether a firm sells software, not
whether it is built on software. By IDC\'s definition the software
market is the revenue earned from licensing or subscribing software to
other organisations, so the firms below fall outside it: each builds
sophisticated software but earns its revenue from a service operated on
that software --- advertising, retail, payments, or transport.[^21]

  Company            Sells                  Revenue source          Sits in
  ------------------ ---------------------- ----------------------- ------------------------
  Baidu              search                 advertising             internet services[^22]
  Naver              search / portal        advertising, commerce   internet services[^23]

The exclusion cuts a second way, however, and the second cut is sharper
than the first. These firms are not software vendors, but they are
*homegrown* platforms that held their domestic market: China, Korea, and
Japan each field a national champion in search, messaging, commerce, and
payments. India\'s equivalents are absent or foreign-owned. Its search,
messaging, and commerce belong to Google, WhatsApp, and Amazon, and its
own attempts --- JustDial, IndiaMART --- are digitised directories,
closer to a printed Yellow Pages than to a platform. Even excused from
the software test, India has no homegrown winner of scale.

### Messaging: the Same Split, One Layer Down {#messaging}

Consumer messaging is not a software-vendor category, but it is the
clearest test of the foundational question, because building a messenger
can yield either a protocol the world adopts or merely an application at
scale.

  App         Origin      What it contributed, and at what layer
  ----------- ----------- --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Signal      US          the Signal Protocol (X3DH key agreement and the Double Ratchet), the end-to-end-encryption standard others adopted[^28]
  Telegram    Russia      MTProto, its own transport-and-cryptography protocol, formally verified but self-designed[^29]
  WhatsApp    US (Meta)   no new layer: built on Erlang/BEAM, Mnesia, FreeBSD, and an ejabberd-derived server, with encryption licensed from the Signal Protocol[^30]
  WeChat      China       a super-app ecosystem at national scale; proprietary, no open protocol (Inferred)
  LINE        Japan       application-layer; \"Letter Sealing\" on standard primitives (Inferred)
  KakaoTalk   Korea       application-layer; near-universal domestic reach (Inferred)
  Hike        India       application-layer; reached unicorn status, shut down in 2021[^31]
  Koo         India       application-layer; shut down in 2024[^32]
  Arattai     India       application-layer, self-hosted; a government-endorsed surge in September 2025 faded within weeks, and end-to-end encryption was added reactively on a conventional scheme[^33]

The pattern is a claim about why consumer software earns adoption at
scale. It is earned in one of two ways: by building something
fundamentally new that others must then build upon, which is to say a
protocol, or by engineering exceptionally well upon open, peer-reviewed
foundations. Signal took the first path, and WhatsApp, Google RCS, and
others now encrypt with the protocol it published rather than reinvent
it. WhatsApp took the second, pouring none of its own floor but standing
on the best open foundations available --- Erlang and its BEAM runtime,
the Mnesia database, FreeBSD, an ejabberd-derived server, and Signal\'s
cryptography --- and engineering brilliantly upon them. Telegram took
the first path more aggressively, building MTProto itself, and is
criticised precisely because it rolled its own cryptography rather than
compose vetted parts. The common thread is exposure rather than secrecy:
a protocol succeeds only by publication and peer review, and great
engineering on open foundations succeeds only by improving software a
competitor is free to inspect and fork. India is absent from both
routes. Its entrants reached the application layer, added nothing back
to the foundations they consumed, and folded.

## The Honest Exceptions {#exceptions}

India has produced real foundational-adjacent software, and an honest
argument must name it. Across this essay it falls into five recurring
shapes, and naming the shape is itself the argument: the **lone
builder**, an unaffiliated individual who rebuilds a tool judged wrong
at the root; the **re-domiciled firm**, genuine floor-level work that
had to become American to raise capital; the **tool trapped in one
firm**, a real primitive built inside a single company and adopted
nowhere else; the **embryonic state project**, institute- or
state-funded, early, and not yet load-bearing; and the **protocol with
no floor**, where India built a rail or a standard but not the systems
software beneath it. The exceptions catalogued below are the first
three; the embryonic state project appears with Sarvam and ShaktiDB, and
the protocol-only shape with the India Stack. None of the five, so far,
becomes a systems primitive the world comes to depend upon.

  Project      What it is                                       Shape                 The catch
  ------------ ------------------------------------------------ --------------------- --------------------------------------------------------
  Calibre      e-book manager, \~500k lines, application        lone builder          one person, \~18 years, unaffiliated[^35]

  Hasura       GraphQL query engine over Postgres, in Haskell   re-domiciled          SF-incorporated, \~US\$136.5mn US venture capital[^37]

  DungBeetle   asynchronous SQL job server, glue                trapped in one firm   Zerodha\'s internal tool[^41]

query engine, YugabyteDB a distributed SQL database, and MinIO an object
store --- genuine floor-level work with Indian founders at its origin
--- and the value and custody of all three now sit in the United States.
India\'s database-layer capability is not absent; it left, and it
clusters in the re-domiciled shape precisely because there was no home
in India it would have stayed for.

# What India Builds, Precisely {#what-india-builds}

Having located India\'s output in the value chain, it is worth examining
precisely what its most celebrated new companies and projects actually
build.

## The Unicorn Factory Is Mostly Non-Software Businesses {#unicorns}

Most of India\'s billion-dollar \"technology\" companies are not
software companies at all. They are conventional businesses --- lenders,
retailers, transporters, gaming operators --- that use software as a
channel to reach a customer.[^43] The engineering inside them is often
genuine, but it is operational engineering on rented foundations rather
than foundational software offered to the world.

  Fintech / lending          Moneyview, KreditBee, Navi   a lender; the balance sheet is the business
  E-commerce / D2C           Zepto, Meesho                retail and distribution with an app
  Logistics                  Porter, Rapido               transport with a dispatch app
  Gaming                     Dream11, MPL                 a real-money gaming operator
  SaaS (application)         Freshworks, BrowserStack     a genuine product firm, at the application layer

## Contribution Does Not Scale With Headcount {#contribution}

India\'s contribution to the world\'s shared code does not track its
headcount. In 2025, contributions to open-source projects based in the
European Union came to more than 934k from the United States, 553k from
the United Kingdom, and 347k from India --- so India, with several times
the United Kingdom\'s developer count, contributed less in absolute
terms.[^44]

Two structural facts explain the gap. Contribution to the foundational
projects themselves is tracked by employer rather than nationality: the
Linux Foundation ranks Intel, Google, Oracle, Red Hat, Meta, and Huawei,
and most Indian-origin systems work is done inside foreign firms and
counted as theirs.[^45] And GitHub systematically undercounts China,
because a large share of Chinese open source lives on the domestic forge
Gitee.[^46] At the level of individuals, the one peer-reviewed measure
puts India at about 5.4% of active open-source contributors, below the
level its income would predict.[^47]

## Krutrim and Sarvam: Real Work, on a Floor Poured Elsewhere {#foundational-ai}

India\'s two most ambitious foundation-model efforts are real and
important, and they are also built entirely upon foundations laid
elsewhere.

                   Krutrim                                   Sarvam
  ---------------- ----------------------------------------- --------------------------------------------------------
  Largest model    12B dense, on a Mistral-NeMo base         105B mixture-of-experts (\~10.3B active), from scratch
  Languages        10                                        22, across 12 scripts

Both run on PyTorch, CUDA, and Linux.[^48][^49] The contrast with
DeepSeek is instructive: the Chinese laboratory contributes
architectural primitives such as its mixture-of-experts design and
multi-head latent attention, and infrastructure tools such as FlashMLA,
that others then adopt, whereas Krutrim and Sarvam optimise the
application layer for a linguistic context the rest of the world will
not serve.[^50] The work is valuable; it is not the floor. Krutrim,
unlike Hasura, is domiciled in India --- *Krutrim SI Designs Private
Limited*, incorporated in Bengaluru in 2023 --- which makes it the
domestic commercial attempt rather than the re-domiciled one, and a firm
that still builds none of the systems floor beneath its models.[^51]

## The India Stack: Protocols, Not Systems Software {#india-stack}

India leads the world in civic digital infrastructure, and it is
important to concede the point plainly before qualifying it. UPI
processes on the order of ₹314 lakh crore a year and accounts for
roughly half of the world\'s real-time payments; Aadhaar has enrolled
some 1.3bn people; MOSIP offers a genuinely open-source reference
implementation of that identity model to other
governments.[^52][^53][^54] These are protocols and public rails, and
they are real. They are not, however, the systems floor --- the
databases, runtimes, and message brokers on which they themselves run,
much of it foreign and hosted on foreign clouds. India\'s civic
infrastructure is world-class at the protocol and application layers,
and it does not extend to the layer beneath.

# What Software Actually Is {#layers}

The argument rests on a distinction between three layers of software,
which it is worth stating precisely.

  Layer                    What it is                                Examples
  ------------------------ ----------------------------------------- -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Application              what a person uses                        wallets, dashboards, office suites
  Middleware               glue between applications and the floor   frameworks, query engines, gateways
  Foundational (systems)   the floor everything runs on              operating systems, databases, message brokers, runtimes, cryptography

The trade term for the bottom row is *systems software*. Other software
depends upon it, and when it fails everything above it fails at once.
Value follows that dependency, because whoever owns the floor sets the
price, the roadmap, the licence, and the shutdown date for everyone
standing on it. A nation strong only in the top row is a tenant, however
prosperous.

# Renting the Floor --- the Empty Middle {#floor}

Before the evidence, the mechanism it is evidence for. Two conditions
must hold together. A wall the builder cannot rent around forces the
floor into existence; an institution independent of the builder keeps it
in existence and lets others adopt it. Remove either and the outcome is
not a floor but one of four familiar failures, each of which has an
Indian name.

consensus systems, operating systems, and cryptography. China\'s column,

# Same Start, Different Choice {#history}

A generation ago China stood roughly where India stands now, assembling
and servicing what others had designed. Ranked on software around the
year 2000, India was ahead, on the strength of English, its services
contracts, and an export head start. What changed was not the starting
line but the direction each country pointed its industry, and India\'s
own attempts at a floor show that the difference was one of direction
rather than capacity.

<div class="story">
<div class="story-label">BOSS — the announcement mistaken for the work</div>
<p>
BOSS, or Bharat Operating System Solutions, is a Debian derivative from C-DAC that has
been promoted since 2007 as India's national Linux. It never achieved adoption at
scale, even within government. Re-skinning Debian is configuring an operating system,
not building one; the floor remained foreign, and only the wallpaper was Indian.
</p>
</div>

<div class="story">
<div class="story-label">Kerala — the forcing function that worked by accident</div>
<p>
Kerala placed free software in its schools from the mid-2000s, through the programme
now known as KITE, because it could not afford proprietary licences. Two decades on,
the state produces a disproportionate share of India's Malayalam-language and
free-software contributors. The forcing function was poverty, as it usually is.
</p>
</div>

# China Built the Floor {#china}

China\'s move into foundational software was driven by strategy and by
siege: the policy of removing IBM servers, Oracle databases, and EMC
storage from critical systems; export controls; and consumer platforms
operating at a scale that purchased Western software could not survive.

The asymmetry deserves stating plainly, because it is what makes these
forcing functions rather than resolutions. China did not select its
walls. Import substitution was a response to a foreign dependency judged
unsafe, export controls were imposed from outside, and a billion
simultaneous users are a fact of demography. A constraint one does not
face cannot be adopted by decision, which is why the instruction to
India is not to emulate China\'s resolve but to find the wall it already
cannot rent around.

<div class="story">
<div class="story-label">The story of OceanBase</div>
<p>
In 2010 Alipay ran on Oracle, and on Singles' Day, the largest shopping event on earth,
the load exceeded what any purchased database was built to survive. Ant Group concluded
that the database itself was wrong for the problem and rebuilt it, in C++, from the
basics, over the better part of a decade.
</p>
<p>
It replaced Oracle in production gradually, first for a tenth of Alipay's traffic and
then for all of it. In October 2019 OceanBase recorded 60.88mn tpmC on the TPC-C
benchmark, taking the record from Oracle's 2010 mark of 30.25mn; in May 2020 it reached
707mn, a result later published at VLDB; and by 2025 Alibaba Cloud's PolarDB had pushed
the record to 2.055bn.[fn:oceanbase][fn:tpcc] A firm that rented Oracle a decade earlier
came to hold the record Oracle once owned.
</p>
<p>
The lesson is not that Chinese engineers are uniquely gifted. It is that a firm which
had the right building blocks made the difficult and riskier choice to build a
foundation rather than buy around the wall. One Western brick even remains: OceanBase's
stored-procedure compiler is built on LLVM. Nobody pours every floor; the point is that
China chose to pour most of it.
</p>
</div>

The same reflex recurred across other firms. ByteDance built the
CloudWeGo microservice frameworks, in Go and Rust, for TikTok-scale
traffic. Huawei shipped the openEuler operating system and the openGauss
database when RHEL became a strategic risk. PingCAP built TiDB, a
distributed SQL database that implements the design in Google\'s Spanner
paper as open source. Between 2015 and 2024, fourteen infrastructure
projects of Chinese origin were donated to the Apache Software
Foundation and now run in production worldwide.[^66]

<div class="story">
<div class="story-label">The story of Gitee</div>
<p>
Not all of it came from giants under siege. Gitee began as a domestic Git-hosting forge
and became China's answer to GitHub — today the state-backed default where a large share
of Chinese open source lives, which is precisely why GitHub-based measures undercount
China. China did not only build its floor through state-mandated champions; it also
repatriated the developer infrastructure itself, an option India has never taken up even
though it operates DigiLocker and a national document-verification system embedded in
the school curriculum, the largest captive market imaginable, to little adoption.
</p>
</div>

# Products vs Services, and Who Contributes the Floor {#products-vs-services}

At the platform layer --- the software that runs private clouds and
telecom cores --- the difference between the two countries is not
capability but posture: China contributes to the source, and India ships
or buys the binaries.

In private cloud, the Chinese firm 99Cloud has been a top-ten global
contributor to OpenStack, while the Indian firm StackUp ships an
OpenStack-based product with little upstream contribution and few large
deployments.[^67] In the telecom core, both countries built a sovereign
stack, which isolates the difference to openness. China Telecom, a
state-owned operator, ran an OpenStack-Powered cloud and co-founded the
Open-O project that merged into ONAP, now a global orchestration
standard; BSNL\'s Bharat stack, built by C-DOT, Tejas, and TCS, is
indigenous but closed and delivered by a systems integrator.[^68][^69]

<div class="story">
<div class="story-label">Author judgement — why the floor stays foreign in India</div>
<p>
India's most software-influential sector is banking, and its largest buyers, the public
and private banks under RBI oversight, take a no-risk posture even in pilots. They
consume open source only through commercial, supported distributions: RHEL rather than
upstream Linux, OpenShift rather than upstream Kubernetes, never Nomad, and never a
domestic newcomer such as StackUp. Several are publicly celebrated for "open-source
excellence" while running exclusively Red Hat's paid, de-risked wrapper of that open
source.[fn:redhatbanks] China's policy excludes foreign platforms and thereby creates
captive demand; India's local-content rules reward badging and are defeated at the point
of purchase by risk aversion.
</p>
<p>
Open-source contribution is itself a trust signal, a public proof of competence that no
sales deck can fake. StackUp contributes little upstream, so a risk-averse buyer has no
such proof and defaults to the vendor that has spent a decade advertising exactly that.
Were StackUp to contribute materially to OpenStack, Indian buyers would likely take it
more seriously — not because the code would be better, but because the credential would
exist (Hypothesis). The absence is self-reinforcing: no contribution, no credibility, no
sales, and no resources with which to contribute.
</p>
</div>

# Comparators: Germany and Russia {#comparators}

China is not the only country that built a floor, and it is not even the
most transferable example, because its delete-everything mandate is
peculiar to its politics. Two other cases show that foundations can be
built under quite different forcing functions, which matters for an
India looking for a route it can actually take.

Germany built foundations through sovereignty rather than siege. SUSE,
one of the oldest commercial Linux distributions, is German;[^70]
LibreOffice is stewarded by The Document Foundation, a German
*Stiftung*;[^71] and Nextcloud, a German company, runs Deutsche
Telekom\'s MagentaCLOUD at 2.2 million users and 7.2 PB under GDPR.[^72]
The forcing function is procurement for digital sovereignty: the state
of Schleswig-Holstein is migrating tens of thousands of public
workstations from Windows and Microsoft Office to Linux and LibreOffice,
explicitly to control its own data, with Fraunhofer institutes and
industry foundations backing the wider effort.[^73] The governance model
is as important as the software, because a foundation held by a neutral
*Stiftung* outlives the firm that began it. This is the most
transferable model to India: procurement for sovereignty, a neutral
custodian, and a slow rather than explosive build.

Russia built foundations organically, and then ran into the limits of
geopolitics. Nginx was written by Igor Sysoev at Rambler and became one
of the most widely deployed web servers in the world, though it is now
owned by the American company F5.[^74] ClickHouse, the column-store
database, originated at Yandex before spinning out as an independent
company that has since relocated. Maintenance, moreover, is a
multi-decade undertaking rather than a launch: Juergen Gross at SUSE
still keeps the kernel\'s Xen and x86 paths alive upstream, a reminder
that custody is a standing obligation, not a one-time act.[^75] The
Russian case is a caution as much as a model: organic invention built
real foundations, but ownership and custody migrated once capital and
geopolitics intervened.

# How Foundations Get Built {#mechanism}

Across the most load-bearing pieces of systems software, five
development patterns recur. It is worth stating them first and then
testing them against the historical record, because the patterns, not
the individual stories, are what India must learn to reproduce.

  \#   Pattern                                                          China instance                                    India instance
  ---- ---------------------------------------------------------------- ------------------------------------------------- ---------------------------------------------------
  1    a lone obsessive rebuilds a tool judged wrong at the root        rare, usually firm-backed                         Goyal, with Calibre and Kitty, unaffiliated
  2    a mass community forms with no single owner                      growing, via the Gitee ecosystem                  thin
  3    an institution commissions a named deliverable for a real user   the delete-IOE mandate produced OceanBase         mis-aimed at services export and defence hardware
  4    a specialist builds the tool their own field lacks               present                                           rare
  5    a scale problem refuses every workaround                         Alipay\'s scale produced OceanBase and RocketMQ   present, but buyers rent around it

The historical record bears the patterns out. SQLite began with a single
engineer on a defence contract before entering the public domain; Linux
began as a student\'s hobby a community adopted; Git and Nginx and Redis
each began with one person meeting a wall that would not yield;
PostgreSQL and Kubernetes began as institutional projects handed to a
community; the Web and the R language were built by specialists who
needed a tool their field lacked; and OceanBase and Hadoop were forced
into being by scale.[^76] Two sources of foundations are conspicuously
absent from that list: the elite teaching institution, and the state
programme aimed at a captive in-house user. Those are the two sources on
which India has leaned hardest.

India\'s state laboratories are the clearest case of the third pattern
mis-aimed. C-DAC, C-DOT, ISRO, BARC, and DRDO produce substantial
software, but it is built for captive institutional use, rarely
open-sourced, and rarely adopted outside the sponsoring agency. The
navigation programme is the sharp example: India built NavIC, the
constellation and the signal, but NavIC reaches consumer devices only
through foreign silicon, having been added by Qualcomm, MediaTek, and
Broadcom to their own chips, while India\'s own navigation chips remain
at the research and space tier. India\'s newest indigenous processor,
C-DAC\'s DHRUV64 --- a 64-bit dual-core RISC-V part released under the
national microprocessor programme late in 2025 --- sits at that same
tier, a test chip at the silicon floor rather than a consumer product,
and, despite occasional loose reporting, a processor and not an
operating system --- an application-class part that runs Linux, taped
out on a 28nm node abroad.[^77] India builds the system and the
protocol; it does not build, and does not get adopted at, the silicon
and systems-software floor.

The supercomputing programme shows the same pattern misfiring at the
level of a single component, and it is the sharpest case because here
the entity *was* Indian. C-DAC did not merely assemble PARAM machines
from imported parts. It designed PARAMNet-3 around its own GEMINI
network co-processor --- a device that offloaded the transport layer
into hardware with direct user-level access to endpoints, supporting
send/receive, RDMA, and datagram services over PCI Express at 10 Gbps,
feeding a 48-port switch at sub-microsecond latency --- and it wrote the
accompanying software stack, Kshipra, described in C-DAC\'s own annual
report as a complete program development environment, with the work
completed across 2007-08.[^78] This was precisely the class of device
whose drivers live upstream in the Linux kernel\'s InfiniBand and RDMA
subsystems, where Mellanox, Chelsio, Myricom, and Quadrics all
maintained mainline support in the same period. \[Inferred from
absence\] No PARAMNet, GEMINI, or Kshipra driver appears in the mainline
kernel. The hardware was retired, the production Supercomputing Mission
systems standardised on Mellanox InfiniBand with Mellanox\'s upstream
driver, and nothing of the Indian effort survives in the shared tree.

The lesson is narrower and harder than the usual one. India built the
interconnect, built the stack to drive it, and kept both inside the
institution --- then abandoned the hardware and rented someone else\'s,
whose driver anyone in the world can read, extend, and depend upon. An
Indian entity owned this work outright, which is the condition the rest
of this essay says is missing, and it still accrued to nothing, because
ownership without publication is a drawer rather than a floor. Entity
nationality is necessary; it is not sufficient. The asset has to be held
in the open for anyone, including India, to build on it later.

# The Kernel Test {#kernel-test}

Most of this essay argues about layers that are hard to audit from
outside. Who really owns a database, who maintains a broker, whose
engineers carry a project --- these are matters of judgement, and a
determined sceptic can contest each one. The Linux kernel removes the
difficulty. It is roughly forty million lines, about sixty percent of
them device drivers, and every line is signed, dated, and attributed in
a public git history.[^79] It is the one place in software where the
question *who built the floor* has a machine-checkable answer.

Run the query for India and the answer is stark. No India-headquartered
vendor maintains a driver family in the mainline kernel, and no core
kernel subsystem --- no filesystem, no scheduler, no subsystem of record
--- originates with an Indian organisation. \[Inferred from absence\]
Indian engineers are abundantly present in the kernel; they are present
as employees of Intel, Qualcomm, Red Hat, IBM, and Google, writing those
companies\' code under those companies\' copyright.

## What an Entity Contribution Looks Like

China supplies the control case, and it does not merely contribute
drivers --- it originates core kernel technology. EROFS, the Enhanced
Read-Only File System, was written by Gao Xiang at Huawei, entered
kernel staging in 4.14 in 2018, was merged to the mainline in Linux 5.4
in 2019, and is now the default read-only filesystem for Android itself,
mandated by Google for Android 13\'s read-only partitions and shipped by
Oppo, Xiaomi, and Samsung; its reviewers now sit at Huawei, Alibaba,
Vivo, and Google.[^80] A Chinese firm authored a filesystem that the
entire Android world runs on, and Huawei\'s name is on it in perpetuity,
in a tree no one can take away from it. Across the 5.x series Huawei
climbed to the top of the per-release contributor tables by changeset
count, reaching first place at 5.8 and again at 5.10.[^81]

That is what an entity contribution looks like: the work is done, the
credit is permanent, the maintainership is held, and the capability
compounds inside an organisation that is not going anywhere.

## The Indian Case, Stated Precisely

The instinctive Indian rebuttal is that Indians do contribute to the
kernel, and it is correct. The rebuttal does not survive contact with
the question of *which entity*.

The clearest instance is kprobes, the kernel\'s dynamic instrumentation
facility, whose base patch was submitted in August 2004 by Prasanna S
Panchamukhi of the IBM Linux Technology Center, India Software Labs,
Bangalore, and which merged to the mainline in 2.6.9 that November.[^82]
Kprobes is genuine core infrastructure --- every tracing and
observability tool in the Linux world stands on it two decades later. It
was originated by an India-based team, at an Indian address, staffed by
Indian engineers.

It is IBM\'s. The copyright is IBM\'s, the maintainership sits within
IBM\'s structure, and the institutional memory of how and why it was
built lives in Armonk rather than Bangalore. The same is true of the
ongoing work from that lab --- tracing, memory management, scheduler and
cgroup bandwidth control --- and of the India-based maintainers at other
foreign firms, such as the dmaengine subsystem maintained from India
under Linaro\'s umbrella. If IBM were to close its Bangalore laboratory
tomorrow, the code would remain in the kernel under IBM\'s name and
nothing of the capability would remain in India as an institution.

This is the essay\'s central distinction reduced to its cleanest form.
Skill was present. Location was Indian. The entity was not, and so
nothing accrued. Kprobes is not a counter-example to the thesis; it is
the exhibit that demonstrates it.

routinely read that way. Read as a statement about national capability
it is a loss statement. The skill was Indian and the asset was foreign
from the first commit, which is the precise sense in which India exports
its foundational software at the moment of creation rather than failing
to produce it.

# What a Foundational Move Requires {#ecosystem}

The origins above name a set of conditions that must travel together for
a foundation to be built and, just as importantly, to endure.

The fourth condition deserves emphasis, because it is the one the
comparators illuminate. A foundation survives its originator only if
some neutral body will take custody of it --- the Apache Software
Foundation, the CNCF, a German *Stiftung*. This is the difference
between MOSIP, which a neutral international setting carried to other
governments, and Drove, which remains excellent and alone in one firm\'s
repository. India\'s failures here are of aim rather than capacity: it
built forcing functions and pointed them at services export, and it
built institutions and pointed them at defence hardware.

MOSIP reached the world because its forcing function arrived with a real
external user, a government that had no identity system, which pulled
the software into the open. That is the condition Drove lacked. India
can build the floor; what it has never built is the institution that
keeps a floor and makes it spread.

The firms that behave most like technology companies are, tellingly, the
ones that produce this rare work. PhonePe repatriated its workloads from
public cloud to its own data centres and built Drove; Zerodha
deliberately chose HashiCorp\'s Nomad over Kubernetes, open-sourced the
glue around it, and runs ERPNext for its own back office. Zerodha\'s
economics make the point concrete: on the order of ₹8,000 crore of
revenue against roughly 1,150 employees, it earns close to US\$870k per
employee, an order of magnitude above the Indian services giants, which
is a technology company\'s leverage rather than a broker\'s. The
capability tracks the technology-company mindset; even so, what it
produces is glue, or a primitive trapped inside one firm.

# The Turn {#turn}

## Elite Institution, Government Grant, and Industry Partnership Have Not Worked {#elite}

India\'s theory of technological progress --- concentrate the ablest in
a few elite institutions, add government grants and a partnership with
an industry leader, and expect breakthroughs --- is aimed against the
mechanism that actually produces foundations. Elite institutions
optimise for individual exit: the graduate who could build a database
has the best exit options in the country, and the trajectory points
outward, or upward into the application layer, rather than toward the
patient and unpaid work of a floor built at home. Meanwhile the services
firms that were meant to anchor the industry are precisely what
artificial intelligence now marks down. The top-down model is losing
both of its engines at once. ShaktiDB (above) is this same configuration
observed in real time, and the scorecard below treats its trajectory as
the cleanest near-term test of whether the pattern still holds.

## Knowledge Is Democratised; the Opening Is Bottom-Up {#msme}

The knowledge required to build foundational software --- the papers,
the reference implementations, the model weights, the tooling --- is now
largely open, and the gate the elite institutions once kept has fallen.
The decisive question is no longer who holds the knowledge but who faces
a real problem with no budget to evade it, which is the forcing function
itself.

  Model                                      Forcing function                    Builders         Governance
  ------------------------------------------ ----------------------------------- ---------------- -----------------
  elite institute and large firm             weak; no real wall                  exit-bound       grant-based
  industrial cluster and local polytechnic   strong; a real problem, no budget   locally rooted   open by default

Kerala produced a version of this outcome by accident, as noted above,
when a lack of licensing money put free software into its schools and
produced a contributor base out of proportion to the state\'s size.

## The One Problem That Is India\'s Alone {#language}

The sharpest target is the problem that artificial intelligence is now
rewriting and that is structurally India\'s to solve: language. India
has twenty-two official languages and about a dozen scripts, and a
billion people whom the current models serve badly. General-purpose
tokenisers fragment Indic scripts so inefficiently that every Indian
model runs slower and costs more to train and serve, a penalty captured
in the token-fertility figures of India\'s own model efforts.[^94] This
is the wall India cannot rent around, and by the mechanism set out at
the start of this essay that is precisely why a floor could be forced
into existence here and almost nowhere else in the Indian economy. No
American or Chinese firm will fix this properly, because the pain is not
theirs; the forcing function is real, domestic, and un-outsourceable,
which is the exact condition that produced foundations everywhere else
in this essay. The pieces already exist, scattered across AI4Bharat,
Sarvam, and Bhashini,[^95] and what is missing is the decision to
consolidate them into a layer beneath every Indian model.

The problem reaches below the model, into the script itself. The
keyboard is a global standard in English, and Indic input has no
comparable settled hardware; even the rendering of Indian scripts on
screen runs on HarfBuzz, the text-shaping engine built substantially
abroad. The layer that draws India\'s own letters was poured elsewhere,
which is the essay\'s argument reduced to a single, uncomfortable
example.

# The One Bet Worth Making {#the-bet}

Everything to this point has been diagnostic. This section is not. If
India were to make a single concentrated attempt at foundational
software --- one target, chosen for the highest probability of
succeeding rather than the highest prestige --- the answer is not an
operating system, not a language, and not a chip. It is the transaction
ledger beneath UPI, built in the open, held by NPCI.

The case is not enthusiasm. It is that this target, alone among the
candidates, already satisfies every condition this essay has argued to
be necessary, and satisfies them without anyone having to manufacture
them first.

## The Condition Nobody Can Manufacture Is Already Met

A foundational primitive requires a wall that cannot be rented around.
Almost every proposed Indian initiative fails at exactly this point: the
problem is real, but a vendor will sell an adequate answer, so the
forcing function never bites. UPI is the exception. In December 2025 the
system processed 21.63 billion transactions worth about ₹27.97 lakh
crore, running near 698 million transactions a day; across FY 2025-26 it
handled on the order of 241 billion transactions against roughly ₹314
lakh crore in value.[^96] That is the largest real-time retail payment
system in the world by transaction count, by a wide margin.

At that volume the economics invert. Commercial database licensing stops
being a line item and becomes a structural cost of the national payment
rail, and failure modes stop being operational and become political ---
the intermittent transaction declines of late March 2025 were front-page
news rather than an incident ticket.[^97] \[Author judgement\] This is
the precise condition under which organisations stop shopping and start
building, because nothing on the shelf fits and no price improves the
fit.

## It Is the Path China Actually Took

The mechanism is already established in this essay. Alipay\'s scale
refused every workaround and OceanBase came out of it --- a distributed
database that went on to take the TPC-C world record and seed an entire
national database sector. That was not industrial policy in the
abstract. It was one payments system at unprecedented scale needing a
ledger that did not exist.

India has the payments system. It does not have the ledger. It runs the
world\'s largest real-time payment network on database and messaging
software it does not own, did not write, and cannot modify. The
structural parallel is exact, and it is the strongest available evidence
that the target is achievable rather than aspirational: the identical
problem, at comparable scale, produced a foundational database once
already, in a country that started further behind.

## The Custody Institution Already Exists

This essay\'s central claim is that India\'s deficit is custody rather
than capability. The reason this target ranks first is that the custody
institution is already built, already neutral, and already sitting at
the right point in the stack.

NPCI was established in 2008 as a not-for-profit company, an initiative
of the Reserve Bank of India and the Indian Banks\' Association under
the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007, owned by a consortium of
banks rather than by any single institution.[^98] \[Author judgement\]
Structurally this is the SITARC model at national scale, and it is the
same shape as an Apache or a CNCF: a member-owned body holding shared
infrastructure that no participant owns individually. Every other
proposal in this space must invent its custodian and then hope the
institution outlives its founders. Here the institution exists, has
operated for nearly two decades, already holds a piece of national
infrastructure, and has the standing to hold a second.

The builders are not exit-bound either. The engineering runs across
NPCI, the member banks --- 694 of them live on UPI as of early 2025 ---
and the payment-service layer around them.[^99] These are institutions
with a permanent stake in the rail continuing to work, which is the
closest thing to a patient builder the Indian software economy has.

## Adoption Is the Hardest Condition, and It Is Solved

What kills most foundational projects is not construction but adoption.
Something gets built, nobody outside the sponsoring organisation runs
it, and it dies as a captive artefact. That is precisely how BOSS and
the state-laboratory software failed, and it is the risk hanging over
ShaktiDB now. A ledger built for UPI does not have that problem, because
UPI itself is being exported: the protocol is going out to other
jurisdictions, and infrastructure that ships with it goes out too. A
primitive with a live domestic user at world-record scale and a ready
international distribution path has already cleared the bar that stops
almost everything else in this essay.

## What Precisely Should Be Built

Not \"a database.\" The target is narrow and nameable: a horizontally
scalable, strictly consistent transaction ledger and settlement engine,
tuned for very high volumes of very small payments, developed in the
open under a permissive licence, with copyright and trademark held by
NPCI or by a foundation NPCI establishes for the purpose. Around it, the
adjacent primitives the same workload forces into existence --- a
durable message log for settlement events, a reconciliation engine, a
schema and wire format for inter-bank instruction. This is the same
cluster of components that grew around OceanBase, and for the same
reason: one workload, hard enough, generates a family of primitives
rather than a single artefact.

The governance requirement is not decorative, and it is the part most
likely to be got wrong, because the PARAMNet case shows what happens
when it is. The work must be open from the first commit rather than
open-sourced on completion; contribution must be possible from outside
the member banks; and custody must sit with the institution rather than
with whichever systems integrator is engaged to build it. Each of those
three has failed before in Indian practice, which is why they are stated
as conditions rather than assumed as defaults.

## The Honest Objection

The strongest argument against this target is not technical and deserves
stating plainly rather than handling. NPCI is a regulated systemic
utility whose first obligation is availability. Utilities under an
availability mandate are correctly risk-averse, and a live national
payment rail is the worst imaginable place to experiment with unproven
infrastructure. The culture that keeps UPI running is close to the
opposite of the culture that builds new systems software.

\[Author judgement\] The answer is that OceanBase did not begin by
replacing Alipay\'s database either. It was built beside the production
system, proved at shadow volume against real traffic for years, and
migrated workload by workload once it had earned the right. That path is
available here, and it is the only responsible version of this proposal:
build alongside, prove at scale without carrying it, migrate
incrementally, and let the production obligation serve as the quality
bar rather than as the reason not to start.

## Why Not the Alternatives

The other candidates are worth building and are weaker as the single
concentrated bet. Indic-language computing has the most defensible
captive problem --- no one else will solve it --- but its output is
mostly models and application tooling rather than systems software, and
its adoption path is largely domestic. The cluster primitives of the
next section have the right mechanism and the right builders, but
operate at a scale where adoption spreads slowly. AI infrastructure has
no forcing function at all while the compute itself is rented. None of
these has a captive problem at world-record scale sitting directly on
top of an existing neutral custodian. That combination exists exactly
once.

open by default, which supplies the governance and the community. It is
the same four conditions, assembled in the places the elite model never
looks.

## The Cluster Is Already a System --- Coimbatore

The reason to name clusters rather than institutes is that a working
cluster is not a list of firms; it is a network with a shared substrate,
and the substrate is where the leverage sits. Coimbatore is the clearest
instance. Its visible face is a directory --- pump makers, foundries in
Ganapathy and Kanuvai, textile machinery, auto components --- but the
structure underneath is a dependency graph. Raw material and casting
feed a machining-and-tooling layer; that layer feeds component
suppliers, who feed module suppliers, who feed OEMs selling into
domestic and export markets. The same tier logic that maps Chennai or
Pune applies here and is almost never drawn for Coimbatore specifically.

The structural point is horizontal rather than vertical. A single
machining shop quotes a pump manufacturer, an auto-component OEM, and a
medical-device firm in the same week; the capability is owned by no one
industry and shared across all of them, which is exactly why the cluster
is resilient --- when textile-machinery demand softens, the same
capacity turns to automotive or renewables. \[Author judgement\] This is
foundational-layer logic expressed in hardware. Value and durability
attach to the shared capability that many verticals depend upon, not to
any single vertical standing on it, which is the same relation systems
software has to the applications above it. A cluster is a floor with
tenants.

What makes Coimbatore load-bearing for this argument is that the cluster
built its own custody institution. SITARC --- the Scientific and
Industrial Testing and Research Centre, established in 1987 --- was set
up by the two industry associations, SIEMA and CODISSIA, as a
not-for-profit registered society independent of any single firm,
NABL-accredited to ISO/IEC 17025, providing testing, calibration, and
metrology to engineering-industry members across India.[^100] Metrology
is the substrate every manufacturer in the cluster depends on and none
of them individually owns: the invisible common floor beneath the
visible machining.

\[Author judgement\] SITARC is to the Coimbatore hardware cluster what a
neutral foundation --- Apache, CNCF, a German *Stiftung* --- is to the
software floor: the independent commons that lets an ecosystem stand on
shared ground rather than on each firm\'s private ground. Read against
the custody argument running through this essay, the point is
uncomfortable and exact. India built the keep-the-floor institution ---
neutral, member-owned, outliving any one participant --- for engineering
metrology, in Coimbatore, in 1987, decades before the question was posed
for software. It has never built the equivalent for systems software.
The talent anchors are in place too: PSG College of Technology,
Kumaraguru College of Technology, and Coimbatore Institute of Technology
supply the engineering base, and machine-tool financing runs through
local banks and NBFCs. Every condition the software floor lacks is
present, in hardware, in one district, assembled by the cluster and held
by no single firm.

The generalisable claim is therefore not that India should fund more
clusters. It is that the cluster already demonstrates the mechanism the
software industry never reproduced: a captive problem, exit-resistant
builders, and --- the piece that matters most --- a neutral institution
that takes custody of the shared floor. The targets in the table above
are worth pursuing because that machine is not hypothetical. It has been
running in Coimbatore since 1987; it has simply never been pointed at a
systems-software primitive.

# Conclusion

The chart that circulates every few months is not wrong about the
numbers; it is wrong about what they mean. India is a large, capable,
application-layer software economy that sells discounted labour. China
is the deeper success, not because it has more engineers or better ones,
but because it chose to own the floor, manufactured the conditions that
force foundations into existence, and continued until it had a stack.
Germany and Russia show that the Chinese mandate is not the only road:
sovereignty procurement and organic invention build floors too, provided
a neutral body takes custody of the result.

The difference was never talent, for India has supplied the world\'s
foundational software with its people for decades, only not at home. It
was direction, and direction is a choice, which means it remains
available.

## Software Worth Building in the Next Twenty Years {#targets}

If India is to build a floor, the targets most likely to reward the
effort share one trait: they are problems the rest of the world has
little reason to solve on India\'s behalf.

-   Indic-language foundational AI, and the tokenisation layer beneath
    it.
-   Offline-first and intermittent-connectivity systems software.
-   Data infrastructure for agricultural and industrial clusters.
-   Systems software close to domestic silicon, as and when a
    fabrication base returns.
-   Sovereign developer infrastructure --- a domestic forge that is
    actually adopted, rather than merely announced.

Twenty years is the horizon, and it is chosen advisedly, because we have
all seen what became of the smart cities announced in 2014.

The floor is already being poured again, one layer up, in the model
weights and the tokeniser on which the next decade\'s software will run,
and the same five shapes are reappearing there. That is the subject of a
companion essay, *Renting the Model*.
