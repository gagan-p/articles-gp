a) *The paradox.* India has 26.7 million developers, US$283bn in annual
   technology revenue, and adds roughly 5.2 million developers a year — yet
   the count of globally adopted foundational software originated and
   governed from India is approximately zero. Capability and outcome have
   never been further apart.

b) *A success that is strategically misfocused.* The Indian software story
   is a genuine success — $283bn revenue, 5.8 million employed. But it is a
   short-term, opportunistic commercial win built on the cost advantage of
   application development and support: India performs ~55% of the world's
   IT outsourcing delivery yet captures ~5.5% of its market value, and holds
   10.5% of world computer-services exports against 0.3% of IP royalties — a
   35× gap between what it delivers and what it owns.

c) *Products built for export, not for home.* India's product and IP
   efforts (Zoho, Freshworks, Postman, BrowserStack) serve export markets
   from the first line of code — domestic revenue is ~10–20%. East Asian
   champions (Alibaba, Tencent, Naver, Kakao) were built domestic-first
   (~70–90% home revenue) and exported only later. India builds for
   problems it does not have.

d) *A different path elsewhere.* Other countries, notably China, treat the
   foundational layer as strategic and pursue import substitution by policy:
   the IOE-replacement drive (IBM, Oracle, EMC) and a captive domestic
   market produced PolarDB Limitless (2,055M tpmC, a 2025 TPC-C world
   record), TDSQL, OceanBase, HarmonyOS, and openEuler — sovereign stacks
   built deliberately, not accidentally.

e) *The floor, and the renter.* This essay abstracts foundational software
   as "the floor" — the load-bearing layer (databases, operating systems,
   consensus systems, orchestrators) on which everything else stands. Every
   player above it is, fundamentally, a renter; and India as an industry is
   a renter.

f) *The exceptions — and how they were built.* India did produce builders,
   but look at /how/ they built. When Ant — a /user/ of databases, drowning
   in Singles' Day load — could no longer rent, it built OceanBase from
   scratch: new storage engine, new consensus, new everything. YugabyteDB,
   a /software company/ rather than a user, took the same problem and
   answered it by assembly: it kept what worked (the PostgreSQL query
   engine, reused almost intact) and built only what was missing (DocDB,
   the distributed storage layer, plus Raft consensus). Zoho built a
   genuine floor but kept it proprietary. PhonePe's Drove is a real
   container orchestrator — a Kubernetes/Mesos-class replacement built when
   Mesos failed PhonePe at scale. Kitty is an exceptional one-person
   terminal; Zerodha's kaf-relay and DungBeetle are production plumbing.
   Real work — but exceptions, not an ecosystem.

g) *Why the exceptions never compound.* The traction of India's
   floor-builders is lost because policy and mindset support none of the
   five patterns by which foundational software gets built — lone
   obsessive, mass community, institutional commission, specialist tool,
   and scale-problem-that-refuses-workarounds ([[#6.2.3][Section
   6.2.3]]). India has no neutral custodian, no long-horizon funding, and
   no domestic forcing function to carry a builder from internal tool to
   global commons.

h) *Missed opportunities.* Government: C-DAC's PARAM supercomputers were
   exported to Germany, the UK and Russia in the 1990s, yet the stack was
   never open-sourced — when PARAM was decommissioned the code went with
   it, leaving roughly zero presence in the Linux kernel. Private:
   Zoho/AdventNet is building its own OS (announced 2023) and built its own database and middleware, but kept the
   floor proprietary — a moat, not a commons; India got a successful
   company, not a foundational ecosystem. Both failures are of governance
   and ecosystem support, not of talent.

i) *Strategy versus Jugaad.* It is not that India never hit the wall.
   PhonePe hit it when Apache Mesos could no longer run its clusters, and
   built Drove to replace it. Zerodha hit it in its reporting layer and
   built DungBeetle. Startups hit it whenever PostgreSQL reaches its
   limits. The characteristic Indian answer is to assemble, extend, or rent
   — recombine what already works — rather than to build the floor from
   scratch. China's answer to the same class of wall was OceanBase: a
   from-scratch, sovereign build. Jugaad wins the quarter; ownership wins
   the decade.

j) *The protocol triumph — and its limit.* UPI (~500 million transactions
   a day, ~₹314 lakh crore a year, roughly half the world's real-time
   payments), Aadhaar (~1.3 billion enrolled), ONDC and ABHA are genuine
   achievements of system-level thinking. But they are a protocol floor
   propped up by policy and mandate, not a technology floor: India defined
   the APIs, standards and identity layers while renting the databases,
   runtimes and message brokers beneath them.

k) *The target.* We propose a Made-in-India stack with a falsifiable
   target: by 2030, at least one globally adopted foundational project
   originated and neutrally governed from India. The approach is
   institutional, not rhetorical — (i) an Indian Open Source Foundation to
   hold IP in neutral custody; (ii) an upstream-first mandate making
   government-funded software (CDAC, CDOT) open by default; (iii)
   MSME-cluster tooling built with local polytechnics on 3–5-year horizons;
   (iv) an Indian-language data corpus; and (v) one 20-year,
   government-commissioned foundational project — a distributed,
   tamper-resistant land-records system, open from day one.

** DONE 3.2. Realization: Chinese DBs Are Ruling TPC
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 3.2
:END:

1. TPC-C measures the *system* — Hardware + OS + DB together. These are
   not standalone database tests; they measure a vertically integrated
   stack.
2. Alibaba's #1 result is a fully sovereign stack — every component
   self-developed:
   - DB: PolarDB for MySQL 8.0
   - OS: Paladin (custom Linux)
   - CPU: Yitian 710 (5nm, 128 ARM cores)
   - Server: Panjiu
   - Network: ICN Switch
3. Tencent's DB, OS, and cluster management are all self-developed.
4. Open source is the *foundation*, not the product. OceanBase is open-
   sourced (Mulan PL); Paladin, Aliyun Linux, tlinux all derive from
   community Linux. But the cluster orchestration, hardware integration,
   and software-hardware co-design are proprietary. The Chinese companies
   are *extending and controlling* open source to build a sovereign floor.

** DONE 3.3. Wonder: Open-Source DBs Are Not There
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 3.3
:END:

Open-source databases are absent because TPC-C submission is expensive:
- Hundreds of nodes of hardware investment.
- Engineering time to tune the full stack: OS, DB, networking, storage.
- An independent audit, adding substantial fees.

No community project has the commercial incentive to spend millions just
to publish a number. This does not mean open-source databases are
untested. The community uses:
- *HammerDB*: open-source TPROC-C/TPROC-H tool; supports PostgreSQL,
  MySQL, Oracle, SQL Server, MariaDB.
- *Sysbench*: multi-threaded benchmark supporting MySQL and PostgreSQL.
- *DBT-2 / DBT-5*: open-source implementations of TPC-C / TPC-E.

Representative HammerDB TPROC-C on single-node AMD EPYC with NVMe:
- PostgreSQL 16: ~600,000 NOPM
- MySQL 8.0: ~400,000 NOPM

The gap is not software capability but the cost of submission — a formal
TPC-C audit runs to millions of dollars. Open-source databases perform
reasonably; they simply do not publish rankings. [Author judgement]

** DONE 3.4. Who Made PolarDB? What Other DBs Have They Made?
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 3.4
:END:

Alibaba was a standard Oracle shop in 2007. Singles’ Day (11.11) —
China’s annual online shopping festival — created a forcing function:
transaction volumes spiked 100× in hours, exposing Oracle’s vertical
scaling limits. Alibaba initiated OceanBase in 2010 (first deployed for

was open-sourced in 2021 under MulanPubL-2.0 and relicensed to Apache-2.0 in March 2026. PolarDB launched in 2018 as a cloud-
native MySQL/PostgreSQL-compatible engine. PolarDB Limitless set the
TPC-C world record in January 2025.

The intent behind this 18-year programme was not to win a benchmark.
Ant now owns its most strategic infrastructure asset end to end —
permanently. The database is not a vendor relationship; it is the
floor. The only remaining risk: a competitor that builds a better
database and anchors superior user experience from the tech side
alone — a risk Ant can at least see coming, because they own the
floor.
#+end_quote

NPCI reports a technical decline rate (system-side failures, not user

errors is ~5–8% depending on the bank. NPCI publishes monthly dashboards
with bank-wise success rates; most PSPs exceed 96% success.

*Q3: What database does UPI use?*

NPCI has not publicly disclosed the database backing the UPI switch.
Industry sources indicate Oracle on IBM infrastructure for the core
clearing system. Participating banks (~300+) use their own core banking
databases — Oracle, SQL Server, and various CBS vendors. There is no
publicly available evidence of an Indian-built database underpinning UPI.

*Q4: Where does the UPI transaction load live — central clearance or
participating nodes?*

UPI load is distributed across the network:
- Authentication (MPIN/UPI PIN): at the PSP bank's servers
- Fund validation and debit/credit: at remitter and beneficiary bank
  core banking systems (~300+ bank nodes)
- Routing, clearing, and settlement: at NPCI's central UPI switch

NPCI central handles routing of all 500M+ daily transactions but the
heavy database operations (account lookup, debit, credit) happen at
individual bank nodes. The central switch is stateless for most flows.
#+end_questions

operations — balance lookup, debit, credit — sit at the ~300 participating
bank nodes. NPCI central routes and settles but does not own the
transaction record. Ant owns every transaction end-to-end on its own
infrastructure; NPCI does not.

#+begin_story
The problem every database faces at scale:
- One machine holds all the data.
- One machine fails, all data is unavailable.
- One machine fills up, the system is stuck.

The solution is distribution. Distribution requires consensus:
agreement on what was written and in what order. Where that
agreement happens is the architectural choice.

*Sharding:* divide the dataset across machines by a key — hash
or range. Each machine owns one subset. Route reads and writes
to the right machine. Replicate each subset for resilience.

Family 1 — Filesystem-level (PolarDB, Aurora, AlloyDB):
- Data stored as pages (B-tree blocks, 8 KB) on a distributed
  filesystem.
- All compute nodes share one storage pool.
- Consensus at the page level — which page, which order.
- One node writes (primary). Multiple nodes read (replicas).
- PolarDB Limitless stacked a sharding coordinator on top to
  break the single-writer ceiling — two architectures combined.

Family 2 — Tablet-level (YugabyteDB, CockroachDB):
- Data sharded into tablets, each replicated across nodes.
- Rows encoded as key-value pairs in RocksDB — a sorted
  log-structured store with no query engine of its own.
- Consensus via Raft: every write confirmed by quorum before
  acknowledgement to the client.
- Both reads and writes scale horizontally.

YugabyteDB's specific choice:
- SQL query layer: PostgreSQL — parser, planner, executor —
  untouched.
- Storage layer: PostgreSQL heap replaced with RocksDB + Raft.
- The engineering work: encoding PostgreSQL rows, types, and
  indexes into RocksDB key-value format.
- Done before PostgreSQL 12's Table Access Method (TAM) API
  existed — required patching PostgreSQL internals directly.
- TAM solves storage abstraction on one node; it does not
  solve distribution.

CockroachDB followed the same family but went further: rewrote
the SQL engine in Go, wrote its own storage engine (Pebble).
More original work; same core insight — leave the solved layer,
attack the gap.
#+end_story

The closest India has come to a database claim in this space is
ShaktiDB — a PostgreSQL 17.4 fork from IITM Pravartak and C-DAC
(Centre for Development of Advanced Computing), under MeitY
(Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology) funding,
released June 2025 (as announced to the author; no public repository
yet). It adds RBI, Cert-In, and SEBI compliance
wrappers on top of community PostgreSQL. It is not a new storage
engine or a new consensus layer. It is a hardened,
compliance-configured fork, deployed by a systems integrator

** DONE 4.3. The Mental Model: Raw Material → Plumbing → Floor → Building → Interior
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 4.3
:END:

To make the argument precise, it helps to have a shared picture of what
software actually is. This essay uses a five-layer metaphor drawn from
construction:

- *Raw Material.* The substrates that everything else is made from:
  operating-system kernels, programming languages, schedulers, build
  systems, runtimes, physical compute primitives (PTY/TTY, PHY,
  buffers), and hardware abstraction layers. These are the sand and
  cement of software.

- *Plumbing and Utilities.* The pipes and wires that connect
  everything: protocols (TCP/IP, HTTP, gRPC), frameworks (Spring,
  React), message queues (Kafka, RabbitMQ), terminals and shells,
  editors, serializers, and the abstractions built around data
  structures such as files, databases, and filesystems. These are not
  the foundation itself, but they are below the visible surface.

- *The Floor.* The load-bearing infrastructure that applications
  stand on: database engines, OS distributions, consensus systems
  (Raft, Paxos implementations), service meshes, and container
  orchestrators. Whoever owns the floor sets the price, the roadmap,
  and the shutdown date for everyone above.

- *The Building.* What a person actually uses: applications,
  dashboards, wallets, office suites, e-commerce platforms.

- *Interior Decoration.* The work that keeps the building habitable:
  support, maintenance, incremental feature addition, customisation,
  and integration. This is where most of India's IT services revenue
  lives.

Dependency runs downward: nothing in the top row executes without every
box in the bottom row. Value capture runs upward: the closer you are to
the raw material, the harder you are to replace and the more of the
value chain you capture. India's named assets attach almost exclusively
to the top two tiers.

** DONE 4.4. Hypothesis Refined: Based on Resource-Based View (RBV)
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 4.4
:END:

Jay Barney’s Resource-Based View: competitive advantage accrues to
assets that are Valuable, Rare, Inimitable and Non-substitutable (VRIN)
— properties that concentrate at the bottom of the software stack. A
database engine qualifies on all four (everything runs on it; few can
build one; a decade to harden; you cannot swap Oracle for a spreadsheet
at scale). A customer-support chatbot is valuable but fails the other
three.

India does not own the VRIN assets in software. It owns the
application layer, which is valuable but not rare. It rents the floor,
paying in licence fees, cloud bills, talent drain, and strategic
vulnerability. The rent is not merely financial. Every year the floor
stays foreign, the domestic capability to build it atrophies further,
because no one learns to pour concrete they can buy ready-mixed.

** DONE 4.5. DPI: The Protocol & Mandate Floor (India’s Parallel Path)
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 4.5
:END:

An honest objection first: India has built world-class digital
infrastructure — UPI (~₹314 lakh crore a year (FY26, NPCI), ~48.5% of the
world’s real-time payments, ACI Worldwide 2023), Aadhaar (~1.39bn
enrolled), ONDC (opening e-commerce protocols at national scale). These
are real and not trivial.

But they are a /protocol floor/, not a /software-engineering floor/.
India built APIs, data standards, and identity layers. It did not
build the databases, runtimes, and message brokers on which those
protocols themselves run. The UPI switch routes 500M+ transactions
daily; the core clearing system is reported to run on Oracle on IBM
infrastructure. The protocol is Indian. The engine beneath it is not.

This distinction is the honest counter-thesis to the essay’s central
claim, and engaging it strengthens rather than weakens the argument.
India’s Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) is world-class at the
protocol and application layers. It does not extend to the layer
beneath. China built the engine /and/ the protocol; India built the
protocol and rented the engine.

- *UPI (Unified Payments Interface).* A real-time payment system
  developed by NPCI that enables instant money transfer between bank
  accounts through mobile devices. It defines the protocol — the
  message format, the authentication flow, the settlement rules. It
  does not define the database that stores the ledger, the operating
  system that runs the servers, or the message broker that queues
  transactions. Those are purchased or rented.

- *ONDC (Open Network for Digital Commerce).* A network-centric
  protocol for e-commerce that aims to break platform monopolies by
  enabling decentralized discovery and transaction. Like UPI, it is a
  set of standards and APIs. The implementation — the search index,
  the payment gateway, the logistics orchestrator — is left to private
  participants, most of whom run on foreign foundational software.

- *ABHA (Ayushman Bharat Health Account).* A free, voluntary 14-digit
  unique health identifier that enables citizens to digitally store,
  manage, and share medical records with verified healthcare providers.
  It is a standard and an identity layer. The electronic health record
  systems that consume ABHA numbers, the databases that store them,
  and the networks that transmit them are built on foreign software
  stacks.

Key references on India’s DPI strategy:
- Tigerfeathers, /”The Internet Country”/ — on how India built
  protocol-level digital infrastructure without owning the software
  floor beneath it.
- iSPIRT Foundation, research papers on the India Stack and DPI
  approach — documenting the strategic choice to build open protocols
  rather than proprietary systems.
- NPCI annual reports and UPI transaction dashboards — for volume and
  technical-decline data.

The DPI argument is not that India built nothing. It is that India
built one kind of floor — protocol and mandate — while omitting the
other kind — software engineering. Both are necessary for strategic
autonomy. Owning the protocol without owning the engine is like owning
the railway gauge but importing the locomotive.

Zoho, known today as a SaaS and enterprise software company, began as
AdventNet in 1996. Founded in New Jersey, USA, by Sridhar Vembu and a small team,
the company started with fifteen people building a Java SNMP (Simple
Network Management Protocol) API. The product was open-source, and it
gained early traction in the network management community.

The SNMP API was followed by WebNMS, an open-source framework for building
network management applications. The early open-source approach gave
AdventNet visibility and credibility, but it was the proprietary products
built on top of that foundation — ManageEngine — that provided sustainable
revenue.

What distinguished AdventNet from most Indian software companies of the
era was that it was building its own stack. The SNMP API, the WebNMS
framework, and later the internal middleware, database, and operating
system were all self-developed. When the dot-com bust arrived in 2000,
AdventNet survived because it owned its floor — it was not dependent on
vendor licenses or third-party platforms that could be withdrawn or
repriced.

The company later pivoted from selling frameworks to selling enterprise
applications (ManageEngine) and then cloud productivity tools (Zoho
Office). The floor — the proprietary middleware, database, and OS —
remained internal. The company grew, but the ecosystem did not. Zoho's
story is covered further in [[#5.2.2][Section 5.2.2]].

AdventNet's early success came from selling network management frameworks.
But frameworks are tools for builders; the real money is in the
applications that builders create. The company gradually pivoted from
selling WebNMS to building its own enterprise applications — first
ManageEngine (IT management) and then Zoho Office (productivity).

The pivot was strategic and successful. Zoho is now a profitable,
privately held company with over 100 million users worldwide (company claim, 2023). It builds its
own OS, database, and middleware — it owns the floor.

But the floor is proprietary. It is not open-source. Zoho does not sell
its database or its middleware as products. The technology that gives
Zoho its cost advantage and operational flexibility is kept internal.

This is the key structural lesson: Zoho built a floor for itself, but it
did not build a floor for India. The company's success is individual, not
systemic. There is no Zoho ecosystem, no Zoho developer community, no Zoho
foundation that holds the IP for the public good. The floor is a moat,
not a commons.

The early open-source approach (Java SNMP API, WebNMS) seeded the company.
The proprietary pivot built the business. But the open-source work was
abandoned, and the proprietary work was not shared. India got a successful
company but not a foundational software ecosystem.

This pattern — build open-source, pivot proprietary, keep the floor to
oneself — is not unique to Zoho. It is one of the shapes of Indian
foundational work examined in [[#6.4.3][Section 6.4.3]].

** DONE 5.3. What Indian Companies Are Actually Building? What Are Other Asian Economies Building?
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 5.3
:END:

India has produced several globally recognised software product companies:
Zoho, Freshworks, Postman, and BrowserStack. They are successful by any
measure — revenue, users, brand recognition. But their success follows a
different pattern from their East Asian counterparts.

The critical distinction is the primary market:

Indian product companies were built for export markets from the first
line of code. Their customers are in the United States and Europe. The
domestic market was not large enough or willing enough to pay for Indian
software products.

East Asian product companies — Tencent, Alibaba, Baidu (China); Rakuten,
Line (Japan); Naver, Kakao (Korea) — were built for their domestic markets
first. They expanded globally only after securing a captive home base.

This distinction matters for foundational software. Building for the
domestic market first creates a forcing function: you must build for local
language, local regulation, local scale, and local infrastructure. These
requirements often cannot be met by off-the-shelf global products. The
domestic market becomes the wall that forces you to build your own
foundation.

Indian product companies, by contrast, build for global customers who
already use global infrastructure. There is no domestic forcing function —
no reason to build a new database, a new operating system, or a new
message broker. The export-first model is efficient for product companies
but it does not generate foundational assets.

The exception proves the rule: Zoho, the essay's own flagship, is
export-first and yet built its own floor (OS, database, middleware).
Zoho's difference was founder discipline and the absence of VC pressure —
a 20-year horizon most export-first companies lack ([[#5.2.2][Section 5.2.2]]).

**** DONE 5.4.2.1. Consumer App Success Routes
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 5.4.2.1
:END:

Consumer applications can succeed via two broad routes. These routes have
different structural consequences for foundational software.

The first route is the protocol route. Define a protocol that becomes a
standard, and build an application that leverages it. WhatsApp used the
Signal Protocol for end-to-end encryption. UPI is a national protocol for
real-time payments. The value is in the protocol, not in the
implementation. The protocol can be adopted by others; the implementation
is a commodity.

The second route is the engineering route. Build an application that is
so well-engineered — performance, user experience, features — that it wins
on merit, using open foundations. Signal built on open-source libraries but
differentiated on engineering quality. Telegram differentiated on speed
and features.

The protocol route creates a floor at the standards level. It defines
how systems interact. But it does not require building the underlying
databases, operating systems, or networking stacks. The protocol is the
floor; the implementation is rented.

The engineering route creates a floor at the implementation level. It
requires deep optimisation of the stack. It often forces the builder to
modify or replace foundational components.

India's consumer successes — UPI, Aadhaar, ONDC — are protocol successes.
They define standards. India has not produced a globally successful
consumer application that is engineering-led in the sense of deep stack
ownership.

#+begin_story
The Unicorn That Rented the Floor

The following is an illustrative, composite account — it amalgamates
several Indian consumer startups, not one named company.

In 2015, a well-funded Indian food delivery startup raised over $500
million from top-tier venture capital firms. It grew to 10 million users,
processed 1 million orders a day, and achieved unicorn valuation in
under three years.

The stack was standard: AWS for compute, MongoDB Atlas for the database,
Kafka on AWS MSK for messaging, React Native for the mobile app, and
Stripe for payments. Everything was rented. The company's "innovation"
was the delivery logistics algorithm — a Node.js service running on
rented servers, querying a rented database, sending messages through a
rented queue.

In 2018, a competitor with a similar product but deeper pockets began a
price war. The unicorn responded by slashing delivery fees. Unit
economics went negative. The investors demanded growth; the company
obliged by expanding to 50 cities, adding 10,000 delivery partners,
and doubling its engineering team.

Then the rent came due. AWS bills tripled as the company scaled.
MongoDB Atlas costs outpaced revenue growth. The logistics algorithm
was bottlenecked by the database, but every proposed fix required
changing the schema — which meant downtime, which meant losing customers
to the competitor. The company could not optimise its stack because it
did not own the stack.

In 2019, the company was acquired for 10% of its peak valuation. The
acquirer shut down the Indian operations, kept the delivery partner
network, and rewrote the logistics algorithm on its own proprietary
infrastructure.

What failed was not the product. What failed was the business model.
The company had no moat. The delivery logistics algorithm was a thin
wrapper around rented infrastructure. The floor is a moat — and they
never owned it.
#+end_story

**** DONE 5.4.2.2. Map Applications
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 5.4.2.2
:END:

Mapping applications provide a useful case study for the "renting the
floor" thesis. Maps are infrastructure: they combine satellite imagery,
street data, traffic patterns, routing algorithms, and user-generated
content. The stack is deep and the data is expensive to collect.

India has several mapping offerings: MapMyIndia (Mappls), Ola Maps, and
ISRO's Bhuvan. Each occupies a different position in the stack.

*MapMyIndia (Mappls):* Privately held, predates Google Maps in India.
Owns its own street data and POI (point of interest) database. But the
underlying infrastructure — the servers, the geocoding engine, the routing
algorithms — is partly built, partly rented. The application layer is
Indian; the floor is mixed.

*Ola Maps:* Built by Ola to reduce dependency on Google Maps. Uses
OpenStreetMap data and self-collected data from Ola's fleet. The routing
engine is proprietary. But the core stack is built on open-source
foundations. Ola Maps is a cost-reduction exercise, not a product sold
to others.

*ISRO Bhuvan:* Government-run, uses ISRO's satellite imagery. The data
is sovereign — India owns its own satellite pictures of its own territory.
But the user interface, the API, and the developer ecosystem are weak.
Bhuvan is a demonstration, not a product.

Contrast with China's Baidu Maps and Russia's Yandex Maps. Both own their
own satellite imagery, street data, traffic data, routing engine, and
developer API ecosystem. The entire stack — data, infrastructure,
application — is sovereign.

The Indian mapping ecosystem is fragmented. MapMyIndia owns data but not
the full infrastructure. Ola Maps owns infrastructure but only for internal
use. ISRO owns satellite data but not the application layer. No single
entity owns the full stack. The floor is rented in pieces.

** DONE 6.1. The Forcing Condition — The Wall
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 6.1
:END:

Foundational software is not built for fun or for strategic planning
documents. It is built when existing solutions fail in a way that cannot
be fixed by paying more money, hiring more consultants, or waiting for
the vendor's next release. There are four kinds of walls:

1. *Cost*. Licensing fees become unsustainable at scale.
2. *Performance*. Off-the-shelf solutions cannot meet latency or
   throughput requirements.
3. *Legal/Sovereignty*. Geopolitical risks or export restrictions force
   domestic alternatives.
4. *Customisation*. The business requires deep integration that
   proprietary vendors cannot provide.

China faced all four walls and answered with greenfield sovereign builds.
India hit the same walls — PhonePe (Mesos), Zerodha (PostgreSQL) — but
answered by assembly and renting, not by building the floor.

| Wall Type | China | India |
|-----------+-------+-------|
| Cost | Oracle licensing at Singles' Day scale was cost-prohibitive | Indian enterprises could afford licenses |
| Performance | Alipay scale exceeded off-the-shelf DB limits; required custom storage engine | UPI's ~3,729 TPS average (2023) — peak >10,000 TPS (NPCI, Oct 2024) — is within PostgreSQL/YugabyteDB capabilities |
| Legal/Sovereignty | US-China trade war forced domestic alternatives | No comparable geopolitical pressure |
| Customisation | Ant needed to modify storage engine; no vendor would accept feature requests | Indian enterprises customise at application layer, not infrastructure |

#+begin_quote
India hit walls, but answered by assembly and renting. China hit walls and
was forced to build greenfield.
#+end_quote

#+begin_story
*SQLite: The Lone Obsessive*

In 2000, D. Richard Hipp was working on a US Navy contract to build a
database for guided missile destroyers. The requirements were strict:
the database had to be small, self-contained, reliable, and work without
a server. Hipp wrote a single C file that implemented a complete SQL
database engine. He released it to the public domain.

SQLite is now the most deployed database on Earth. It runs in every
Android phone, every iPhone, every web browser (via WebSQL), and
billions of embedded devices. The code is still maintained primarily by
Hipp and a small team.

What made SQLite possible was not a large corporation or a government
mandate. It was a single developer with a clear problem, deep expertise,
and the discipline to keep the code simple. The US Navy provided the
funding and the requirement; Hipp provided the execution.

SQLite exemplifies the “lone obsessive” pattern: a single individual
with a clear vision and the freedom to execute it.
#+end_story

#+begin_story
*PostgreSQL: Institutional + Community*

In 1986, Michael Stonebraker at UC Berkeley received DARPA funding to
build a database system that would extend the relational model beyond
simple data types. The project was called POSTGRES. It introduced
concepts like object-relational extensions, rule systems, and
procedural languages.

The research produced a working system, but the university did not
commercialise it. The code was released to the community. A group of
developers formed the PostgreSQL Global Development Group and continued
development, transforming it into a production-grade database.

PostgreSQL is now one of the most advanced open-source databases in the
world. It powers millions of applications, from startups to
enterprises. The community is large and distributed, but the core
governance remains meritocratic.

PostgreSQL exemplifies the “institutional + community” pattern: a
research project that outlived its funding by becoming a community
asset.
#+end_story

| Mass community | Large distributed community grows around shared need, often corporate-backed | Linux, Apache httpd |
| Institutional commission | Corporation, government, or university funds development for internal use | Kubernetes, OceanBase, TCP/IP |
| Specialist tool | Built to solve specific niche problem; often by company or researcher | Nginx, LLVM |
| Scale problem that refuses workarounds | Problem so large that no existing solution works, forcing new build | OceanBase (Singles' Day), Hadoop |

#+begin_story
*Kubernetes and OceanBase: Institutional Commission*

Kubernetes began as Google’s internal cluster management system, Borg.
Google had a scale problem: millions of containers, petabytes of data,
and thousands of developers. No existing solution worked. Google built
Borg, then open-sourced Kubernetes. The project was donated to the CNCF,
a neutral foundation, ensuring global adoption.

OceanBase began inside Alibaba in 2010; Ant Financial adopted it later. The Singles’ Day load broke Oracle’s
vertical scaling limits. Ant built a distributed database from scratch.
The project was open-sourced in 2021 under MulanPubL-2.0 (relicensed Apache-2.0, March 2026).

Both fit the institutional-commission pattern: a scale problem no vendor
could solve, then open-sourcing to win community adoption.

The parallel to India is stark. Indian corporations with scale problems
—NPCI, Zerodha, PhonePe—have built internal tools (Drove, kaf-relay,
DungBeetle), but they have not donated them to neutral foundations or
built communities around them. The institutional commission pattern
exists in India, but it stops at the internal-use stage.

** DONE 6.7. Story Box: PhonePe Drove
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 6.7
:END:

#+begin_story
*PhonePe Drove: Foundational Work, External Silence*

PhonePe processes billions of transactions a year. When Apache Mesos
could no longer run its clusters, PhonePe built Drove — a container
orchestrator written from scratch for that scale. This is not an
application. This is foundational work: a NUMA-aware scheduler that pins
containers to cores so they stop stepping on each other, controller
leader-election, automatic zombie-container detection (which Mesos left
to the SRE team to kill by hand), and a two-component design — controller
and executor — small enough that a small team can run the whole cluster.

Kubernetes keeps the truth of its cluster state in etcd. Drove keeps its
truth in Apache ZooKeeper — state persistence, leader election, and the
store for every application and task specification. An application in
Drove is a real state machine (=INIT= → =MONITORING= → =RUNNING= →
=OUTAGE_DETECTED= → … → =DESTROYED=), not a pile of YAML reconciled by hope.

Look closer at what Drove does /not/ do, and the gap between
"foundational" and "finished" shows:

- No auto-scaling — PhonePe runs a proprietary external auto-scaler.
- No network policy — traffic control happens at the VRF level.
- No multi-DC cluster — the docs admit one was never tested.
- No end-to-end configuration management — config is maintained outside
  the cluster, under PhonePe's own approval workflows.
- Observability is a real-time event stream plus raw metrics on admin
  ports; you assemble the stack yourself from Telegraf, a modified
  OpenTSDB, and Grafana.

The point is not that Drove is weak. The point is that this is exactly
the kind of foundational work India is told it cannot do — built by a
payments company in the margins of its core business, released under
Apache 2.0, and then left to coast. Low external adoption, few outside
PRs, no foundation, no ecosystem. Open-sourced, not community-built.
(Drove's internals deserve a dedicated note; this essay only needs the
lesson.)

India's companies are good at building code. They are not yet good at
building communities.
#+end_story

** DONE 6.8. Today: CDAC and IITM Database and OS Attempts

#+begin_story
*Zepto and the Upstream PR*

The following is an illustrative account [Author judgement] — no public
Zepto engineering post or PostgreSQL commit confirms this specific PR.

Zepto, a fast-growing quick-commerce startup in India, faced a
database bottleneck. Their PostgreSQL setup was struggling with the
volume of concurrent writes. The engineering team evaluated options:
build a custom fork of PostgreSQL, rewrite the storage engine, or
change the schema.

They chose the most efficient path: they filed a PR upstream to
PostgreSQL, adding a feature they needed. The PR was accepted. Zepto's
problem was solved without building anything new.

This is rational engineering. It is the best use of resources in the
short term. But it is also the perfect anti-pattern of “renting.” Zepto
solved its problem, but it did not build a strategic asset. The
solution is owned by the PostgreSQL community, not by Zepto or by India.

If every Indian company does the same—submit PRs upstream, never build
their own—India will always be a consumer of foundational software,
never a producer. The efficiency of the short term is the trap of the
long term.

The counter-example is Ant Group. Ant faced a similar problem with
Oracle. They did not submit a PR to Oracle. They built OceanBase from
scratch. The initial cost was higher, but the long-term benefit was
strategic ownership.
#+end_story

** DONE 7.1. Our Innovation Ecosystem Is Not Working
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 7.1
:END:

India's innovation system has three actors: government research institutes
(CDAC, CDOT, CSIR), elite academic institutes (IITs, IISc), and industry
(IT services firms, product startups). None of the three has a 20-year
horizon for foundational software. The result is systemic misalignment.

Government institutes build for government procurement. Success is measured
by contracts signed and budgets consumed, not by adoption or community
growth. Elite academic institutes publish papers and graduate PhD students
who leave for Google and Meta. Industry optimises for quarterly margins and
builds only what clients pay for. The triad has no feedback loop that rewards
foundational investment.

The Chinese triad works differently. State policy sets the direction (IOE
replacement). State-owned enterprises provide the market (captive demand).
Research institutes provide talent that stays domestic because the market is
large enough. The feedback loop is closed. India's is open at every joint.

What India is missing is not any one actor — it is the connective tissue: a
neutral institution that can hold IP, govern a community, absorb talent from
all three actors, and maintain strategic patience across political cycles
(developed in [[#7.3][Section 7.3]]).

*** DONE 7.2.1. Case for SME Medium-Term
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 7.2.1

The forcing function does not have to be national. It can be local.

India's MSME clusters — textile mills in Surat, auto-parts suppliers in Pune,
kirana distributors in Coimbatore — face real software problems that no
enterprise vendor will solve: inventory systems that work in Hindi, logistics
tools that handle cash-on-delivery edge cases, compliance reporting for GST
at volumes too small for SAP and too complex for a spreadsheet.

These are solvable problems. A local polytechnic, given a 3–5 year grant
tied to a specific cluster's problem, can do this — students get
real-world engineering; the cluster gets working software; if it is
open-sourced, every similar cluster benefits.

Not a database. Not an OS. But it is how the lone obsessive pattern
starts: one polytechnic, one cluster, one problem.

*** DONE 7.2.2. Case for Government-Supported Very Long-Term
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 7.2.2
:END:

Some problems require a 20-year timeline and cannot be left to the market.
DARPA funded TCP/IP. The US government funded the Human Genome Project.
Germany’s Sovereign Tech Fund pays open-source maintainers to keep critical
infrastructure alive. These are not market failures — they are problems the
market will never solve because the returns are diffuse and the horizon is too
long.

India has one candidate problem that meets this bar: a distributed,
tamper-resistant land records system. Land title disputes consume 66% of
India’s civil court dockets. No commercial vendor will solve it because
there is no paying customer — the government is the only customer, and it
has historically preferred renting (proprietary state-vendor databases) over
building.

The model: fund an open project under a neutral foundation, similar to how
NPCI was structured for UPI but with open source from day one. Mandate that
all state-government land record systems implement the open protocol. A
working prototype in five years, production rollout in ten, full ecosystem
in twenty.

The alternative is to continue renting foreign database vendors to store
India’s most politically sensitive data — which is the current situation.

** DONE 7.3. Go Back to Zoho’s Case
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 7.3
:END:

Zoho is the clearest Indian proof that owning the plumbing compounds.

AdventNet built WebNMS — a proprietary network management framework — in
the late 1990s. That single foundational investment seeded ManageEngine,
which today competes globally with ServiceNow and SolarWinds. When cloud
vendors raised prices, Zoho was insulated because it ran its own
infrastructure. When competitors depended on Salesforce for CRM, Zoho
had built its own.

Owning the plumbing is not about open source versus proprietary. It is about
strategic depth. Each layer Zoho built internally became a moat that compounded
over twenty years.

The lesson is that one layer of foundational ownership, maintained with discipline over
a decade, changes the strategic position of everything built on top of it.

Hasura built a world-class GraphQL engine. The founders were Indian. The
initial engineering happened in India. Then they incorporated in the US,
raised venture capital in the US, and scaled globally from the US. Hasura
is now a US company that happens to have Indian engineers.

This was not a failure of ambition or capability. It was a rational response
to structural incentives. Indian VCs did not fund deep-tech at Series A.
Indian enterprises did not buy from Indian companies without a US entity as
counterpart. The US Delaware C-Corp was the only path to capital and
customers.

A neutral custody institution — call it an Indian Open Source Foundation —
could change this calculus. If such a body could hold Hasura’s IP under a
neutral licence, provide credibility to enterprise buyers (the way Apache
membership does), and allow founders to raise capital from any jurisdiction
while keeping the strategic asset Indian, re-domiciliation becomes unnecessary.

The model exists. Apache does this for Kafka, for RocketMQ, for hundreds of
projects. CNCF does it for Kubernetes. India needs one institution of this
kind — not a government body, not a company, but a genuine neutral foundation
with legal standing, enterprise credibility, and a long-term mandate.

** DONE 7.4. Establish: We Failed Not Because of Talent, But Because of Governance
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: 7.4
:END:

Neha Narkhede co-created Apache Kafka at LinkedIn. Vikram Adve
co-created LLVM at UIUC. Indian engineers at Google, Meta, and
Microsoft have authored critical subsystems of Android, PyTorch, and
the Linux kernel. The talent exists. It built the floor — for American
companies, funded by American institutions.

The governance failure is specific:

1. *No custodial institution* to hold IP and maintain neutrality ([[#7.3][Section 7.3]]).
2. *No long-term funding mechanism* (India has no DARPA equivalent for software).
3. *No strategic patience* — government technology initiatives rarely survive
   a change of minister.
4. *No domestic market forcing function* — Indian companies rent, so Indian
   talent has no local problem to solve at scale.

The argument "India has talent" is true and irrelevant. The question is
whether India has the institutions to translate that talent into strategic
assets. It does not, yet.
